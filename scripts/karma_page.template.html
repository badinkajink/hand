KaRMA rolling-pinch metric on the real_v1 design family

KaRMA, a published kinematic score of thumb–index rolling-pinch dexterity, was computed for {{N_DESIGNS}} real_v1 designs labelled by this program’s retention screen. It predicts which hands keep the screwdriver at AUC {{AUC_T}}, {{DELTA}} above the best distance measurable off the mount plate, and ranks the residual clip a hand absorbs at ρ {{RHO_CLIP_T}}. It says almost nothing about how far the hands turn the tool: ρ {{RHO_T}} against the screened turn, and AUC {{AUC_CONF_T}} for confirmed against retained designs. Within this one topology the score spans {{SPREAD_RATIO}}×, and it changes with the choice of finger pair.

Terms and metrics

KaRMA-T, KaRMA-R, KaRMA-S
Reachable object-centre volume of the rolling pinch; share of 228 twist-invariant orientation bins reached; dependence of the result on the initial pinch. All three are dimensionless.
L_ref
The characteristic hand size by which KaRMA scales every length, in mm.
Retained, confirmed
Designs that pass the retention screen (60 mm proof lift, then 5 s of free hold) at some residual clip; retained designs that pass its repeat and full-error stages.
Residual clip
Largest joint offset of a plan from its grasp anchor, in rad; under the retention screen a larger clip is the harder test.
AUC
Area under the ROC curve for separating two groups by a score; 0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect.
ρ
Spearman rank correlation.
Screened turn
Signed cosine of the tool’s axis with vertical after the screened plan’s turn; +1 is upright.
Mount distance
Straight-line distance between the two scored finger mounts, in mm.
D1 to D8
The eight hands deployed to the bench.

KaRMA and the real_v1 family

KaRMA (arXiv:2605.15548, Peticco and Agrawal, IROS 2026) puts a hand in a thumb–index precision pinch on a sphere and searches, by breadth-first expansion over translation and rotation primitives, for every object pose the pinch can roll to without letting go. Each step enforces joint limits, self- and object collision, a rolling-contact constraint solved as a QP, and antipodal force feasibility. It reports three dimensionless numbers: KaRMA-T, the reachable object-centre volume; KaRMA-R, the fraction of 228 twist-invariant orientation bins reached; and KaRMA-S, how much the answer depends on which initial pinch you start from. Every length is scaled by a characteristic hand size L_ref, so the scores are exactly invariant to rescaling a hand uniformly.

The authors describe it as a standardized lower bound on thumb–index rolling-pinch dexterity for procurement and design iteration, and exclude task success from its claims. The test here is whether it ranks this program’s candidate hands usefully.

real_v1 makes that test clean. Every hand has the same fingers (links of 33.45, 33.45 and 37.16 mm, the same joint limits), and a design is six numbers, the x and y of each finger’s mount. KaRMA is therefore a three-number function of those six. On the same label, logistic regression on the six mount coordinates predicts retention at AUC 0.839, the grasp fit’s depth_fit at 0.803, and the extension ceiling of the rotational-lock page at 0.617 (n = 80).

Export of the hand to KaRMA and its checks

KaRMA reads a URDF, and real_v1 is written in MJCF. scripts/karma_export_urdf.py reads a real_v1 MJCF and writes the URDF plus the per-hand YAML. Two details set what the score means.

tip_length_m is the distance from the terminal joint origin to the outer surface of the fingertip, because KaRMA subtracts its own capsule radius from that value to get the medial axis. For real_v1 that is 37.16 mm, the CAD LINK_DISTAL, and the capsule end then meets the fingertip surface of the MJCF pad sphere to the micron. Palm height is dropped: KaRMA canonicalizes the base link to the world origin, so a rigid translation cannot change the score, and each real_v1 design has its own palm height from fit_real_v1_pose.py.

The export was checked three ways. The install reproduces the paper’s Inspire row exactly (4 voxels, KaRMA-T 0.000417, KaRMA-R 0.004386, KaRMA-S 0.2500, L_ref 155.4 mm); exporting a design from its own MJCF and re-mounting the base hand at its six coordinates give byte-identical URDFs on 20 of 20 designs; and pinning karma.seed_selection to one process, which stops 18 concurrent runs from thrashing, returns bit-identical scores on the base hand.

Prediction of retention

{{CHART_AUC}}

KaRMA-T separates retained from discarded designs at AUC {{AUC_T}} (bootstrap {{AUC_T_CI}}) and KaRMA-R at {{AUC_R}}. The best single number measurable off the mount plate, how close the two scored mounts are, reads {{AUC_RULER}}. A paired bootstrap over the same designs puts KaRMA-T ahead of it by {{DELTA}} ({{DELTA_CI}}, p < 0.001). Logistic regression on all six mount coordinates reads {{AUC_MOUNTS}} here, and adding KaRMA’s three scores takes it to {{AUC_BOTH}}. The search adds 0.07 to 0.09 of AUC to the geometry it is computed from, at about 220 core-seconds per hand.

KaRMA-S is inverted. At AUC {{AUC_S}} a design whose score depends heavily on which pinch you start from is less likely to be retained, so on this family the sign of the sensitivity score matters as well as its size.

How much residual clip a hand absorbs

Binary retention is the coarsest form of the label. The screen also records the highest residual clip at which each design still passes, and under this gate a bigger clip is the harder test: 305 designs pass at 0.50 rad and 33 at 1.30, because a larger turn excursion costs grip before the proof lift. Against that ordinal, KaRMA-T reads ρ {{RHO_CLIP_T}} (p = {{RHO_CLIP_T_P}}, n = {{N_CLIP}}) and KaRMA-R {{RHO_CLIP_R}} (p = {{RHO_CLIP_R_P}}). So the metric ranks how much residual authority a hand can absorb and still keep the tool.

The screened turn

Over the {{N_TURN}} designs that produced a screened turn, KaRMA-T correlates with the turn’s signed cosine at ρ {{RHO_T}} (p {{RHO_T_P}}) and KaRMA-R at {{RHO_R}} (p {{RHO_R_P}}). Among the retained designs, separating the {{N_CONF}} that went on to be confirmed runs at AUC {{AUC_CONF_T}} for KaRMA-T and {{AUC_CONF_R}} for KaRMA-R. The metric ranks which hands hold the tool and says almost nothing about how far they turn it, the quantity this program optimizes and the authors exclude from the metric’s claims.

Choice of the finger pair

KaRMA takes a thumb and an index. real_v1 has three fingers, and this programme’s own result is that the third loaded finger keeps the tool. The opposed pair peaks at 0.995 and drops the tool in every rollout with the thumb at 0 N, while inline-plus-thumb ends at 0.884 and keeps it on three loaded fingers. Scoring the same {{PAIR_N}} designs with thumb–index and again with thumb–middle gives Spearman {{PAIR_RHO}} between the two rankings, and a median absolute disagreement of {{PAIR_RATIO}} in log₂ KaRMA-T.

{{CHART_PAIRS}}

On the {{PAIR_N}} designs scored both ways, thumb–index predicts retention at AUC {{PM_TI}} and thumb–middle at {{PM_TM}}; the paired difference is {{PM_DELTA}}. At this sample size neither pair is demonstrably the better one to give the metric. Taking the better of the two scores per design reads {{PM_BEST}}, no better. A three-finger hand therefore has no single KaRMA score: the number depends on the choice of pair.

Relation to mount distance

KaRMA-T is Spearman {{RHO_GEOM}} with the straight-line distance between the two scored mounts, over {{N_DESIGNS}} designs, and {{RHO_LREF}} with L_ref itself. Its ranking is close to “compact hands score higher”, which is what this programme’s funnel already selects: the retained population is more compact than the sampled one in both x_sep (105.2→91.4 mm) and y_sep (110.0→99.8 mm). The {{DELTA}} it adds over that distance is the part of its answer the ruler does not contain.

Scale invariance

KaRMA scales every length by L_ref, which suits comparing hands of different sizes but ignores the fixed 25 mm shaft. Re-running {{SC_N}} designs with the factor set to 1, the sphere at the shaft’s 12.5 mm radius and the capsule at the real 10.55 mm pad, leaves the KaRMA-T ranking at Spearman {{SC_RHO}}. It moves retention AUC by {{SC_DELTA}} and leaves the turn correlation at {{SC_TURN}}.

The invariance therefore does not explain the flat turn correlation; recovering the turn would take a change to the search itself.

KaRMA’s pinch against the hand’s grasp

KaRMA chooses its own pinch, ranking every feasible seed by a trial search. Over {{N_DESIGNS}} designs it chose a median depth of {{DEPTH_MED}} mm below the mounting plane. The eight deployed plans grasp at 50.5–66.5 mm.

{{CHART_DEPTH}}

The contact model differs too. KaRMA models every finger link as a capsule of one radius (11.8 mm nominal, 11.37 mm after scaling on this hand) and allows contact anywhere along it. real_v1’s links are 10.55 mm capsules with a pad sphere at the tip, and the pad is the only surface carrying the high-friction contact parameters. Rebuilding each hand in MuJoCo at KaRMA’s own seed angles, with a sphere of KaRMA’s own radius at KaRMA’s own seed centre, the contact fingers sit a median {{GAP_MED}} mm from the object on the hardware geometry, and {{PAD_PCT}}% of those contacts are nearest at the pad; the rest lie partway up a link.

{{FIG_REPLAY}}

Driving KaRMA’s own joint trajectory through MuJoCo, on KaRMA’s own capsule geometry with its own sphere at its own seed pose, does not hold the object. The pinch has no preload by construction (the search drives the contact gap to within eps_g and no further), so nothing presses. Adding 5° of commanded squeeze on the two contact fingers does not fix it. The sphere is never held at two contacts and normal force stays at zero. It travels {{REPLAY_RATIO}}× the predicted distance because it is pushed and does not roll.

KaRMA delivers the kinematically feasible rolling path it claims. Executed open loop under position control the path drops the object, so using it as a motion primitive needs added grip regulation.

The eight deployed hands

The eight were selected in simulated-cosine rank order, so any variable correlated with the selection correlates with the outcome inside the set (depth_fit against simulated cosine reads −0.90 over these eight and +0.076 over the 535 retained); they are reported apart from the population.

KaRMA spreads the eight over {{DEP_RANGE}}× in KaRMA-T. Against their screened turn it reads ρ {{DEP_RHO}} (p {{DEP_P}}, n = {{DEP_N}}), which is what the paragraph above says to disregard. The one thing worth recording is positional: D7, the reference configuration and the only one that has run the whole chain end to end, is the highest-scoring of the eight, and D6, the hand that completes the chain at pivot 0.15, sits mid-pack.

Future work

  1. Tip length. Re-run the 128-design subset with karma_export_urdf.py --tip-mode centre (capsule ending at the pad centre, 10.55 mm shorter, about 8 core-hours); the margin of {{DELTA}} over the mount distance should survive.
  2. Gain over the mounts. Bootstrap the 5-fold fits (200 resamples, n = {{N_DESIGNS}}) to give {{AUC_BOTH}} for mounts and KaRMA against {{AUC_MOUNTS}} for the mounts alone an interval.
  3. Designs where KaRMA and the screen disagree. Fit CEM grasps for the ten highest-scoring designs the screen rejected and ten low-scoring controls of equal mount compactness (fit_real_v1_pose.py, then probe_real_v1_carry.py --repeats 8 --jitter 0.0005 --linear-anchor); KaRMA finds hands the screen missed only if the first group holds more often.
  4. The other two topologies. Extend karma_export_urdf.py to the nine-parameter MJCF and score the inline (m05) and opposed (perp) hands; a two-finger metric that prefers the opposed pair, which drops the tool in every rollout, would mislead a topology choice.
  5. Hardware validation of the screen. Eleven of the sixteen best hands of the clip rescreen keep the tool at no clip on the bench schedule, so validate the screen on the bench before a KaRMA score picks a hand to build.
