The KaRMA kinematic metric on the real_v1 design family

KaRMA scores a hand by how far a two-finger pinch can roll a sphere without releasing it, from kinematics alone. This runs it over {{N_DESIGNS}} real_v1 designs that already carry retention labels from this programme’s own screen, and asks the two questions that decide whether it is worth adopting: does it separate hands inside one topology, and does it predict which of them keep the screwdriver.

What KaRMA measures, and why this family can test it

KaRMA (arXiv:2605.15548, Peticco and Agrawal, IROS 2026) puts a hand in a thumb–index precision pinch on a sphere and searches, by breadth-first expansion over translation and rotation primitives, for every object pose the pinch can roll to without letting go. Each step enforces joint limits, self- and object collision, a rolling-contact constraint solved as a QP, and antipodal force feasibility. It reports three dimensionless numbers: KaRMA-T, the reachable object-centre volume; KaRMA-R, the fraction of 228 twist-invariant orientation bins reached; and KaRMA-S, how much the answer depends on which initial pinch you start from. Every length is scaled by a characteristic hand size L_ref, so the scores are exactly invariant to rescaling a hand uniformly.

The authors are explicit that it is “a standardized lower bound on thumb–index rolling-pinch dexterity, not a universal dexterity score, and not a predictor of task success”, and that it is meant for procurement and design iteration. Design iteration is exactly what this programme does, so the useful test is not whether KaRMA is a good metric in general but whether it ranks our candidate hands usefully.

real_v1 makes that test unusually clean. Every hand in the family has identical fingers — three links of 33.45 / 33.45 / 37.16 mm, three revolute joints, the same limits — and the design space is six numbers, the palm-frame x and y of each finger’s mount. KaRMA is therefore a three-number function of those six, and cannot carry information the six do not. The question is whether its compression is a useful one. There are published incumbents to compare against on the same label: logistic regression on the six raw mount coordinates predicts retention at AUC 0.839 and the grasp fit’s depth_fit at 0.803, while the one purely kinematic geometric predictor this programme has tried — the extension ceiling asin(extend/straddle) — reached AUC 0.617 at n = 80.

Porting the hand, and the checks that it ported correctly

KaRMA reads a URDF; real_v1 lives in MJCF. scripts/karma_export_urdf.py reads a real_v1 MJCF and writes the URDF plus the per-hand YAML. Two details set what the score means.

tip_length_m is the distance from the terminal joint origin to the outer surface of the fingertip, because KaRMA subtracts its own capsule radius from that value to get the medial axis. For real_v1 that is 37.16 mm, the CAD LINK_DISTAL, and the resulting capsule end lands on the same fingertip surface as the MJCF pad sphere to the micron. Palm height is dropped: KaRMA canonicalizes the base link to the world origin, so a rigid translation cannot change the score, and each real_v1 design carries its own palm height from fit_real_v1_pose.py.

What was verified before any number was believed
  • The install reproduces the paper. Inspire returns 4 voxels, KaRMA-T 0.000417, KaRMA-R 0.004386, KaRMA-S 0.2500, L_ref 155.4 mm — the published row exactly.
  • The two export routes agree. Reading a generated design’s own MJCF and re-mounting the base hand at that design’s six coordinates give byte-identical URDFs on 20 of 20 sampled designs.
  • Running one metric process instead of ten changes nothing. karma.seed_selection sizes its own process pool from the core count, independently of rotation_workers, which made 18 concurrent runs thrash; pinning it to one process returns 46 voxels, KaRMA-T 0.005667, KaRMA-R 0.1652, KaRMA-S 0.4348 on the base hand, bit-identical to the shipped path.

It separates hands inside one topology

Across {{N_DESIGNS}} designs that differ only in where three fingers are bolted to a plate, KaRMA-T spans {{SPREAD_RATIO}}× between the best and the worst. The sixteen commercial hands in the paper’s own table span {{PUB_RATIO}}×. The median real_v1 hand sits at the {{PCTILE}}th percentile of those sixteen.

It does not predict which hands keep the tool

KaRMA-T separates retained from discarded designs at AUC {{AUC_T}} (bootstrap {{AUC_T_CI}}) and KaRMA-R at {{AUC_R}}. The best single number measurable off the mount plate, how close the two scored mounts are, reads {{AUC_RULER}}; a paired bootstrap over the same designs puts KaRMA-T ahead of it by {{DELTA}} ({{DELTA_CI}}, p < 0.001). Logistic regression on all six mount coordinates reads {{AUC_MOUNTS}} here, and adding KaRMA’s three scores takes it to {{AUC_BOTH}}. The search buys roughly 0.07 to 0.09 of AUC over the geometry it is computed from, at about 220 core-seconds per hand.

KaRMA-S is inverted. At AUC {{AUC_S}} a design whose score depends heavily on which pinch you start from is less likely to be retained, so the sensitivity score carries sign as well as magnitude on this family.

How robustly it holds

Binary retention is the coarsest form of the label. The screen also records the highest residual clip at which each design still passes, and under this gate a bigger clip is the harder test, not the easier one: 305 designs pass at 0.50 rad and 33 at 1.30, because a larger turn excursion costs grip before the proof lift. Against that ordinal, KaRMA-T reads ρ {{RHO_CLIP_T}} (p = {{RHO_CLIP_T_P}}, n = {{N_CLIP}}) and KaRMA-R {{RHO_CLIP_R}} (p = {{RHO_CLIP_R_P}}). So the metric ranks how much residual authority a hand can absorb and still keep the tool.

Over the {{N_TURN}} designs that produced a screened turn, KaRMA-T correlates with the turn’s signed cosine at ρ {{RHO_T}} (p {{RHO_T_P}}) and KaRMA-R at {{RHO_R}} (p {{RHO_R_P}}). The metric ranks which hands hold the tool, and says almost nothing about how far they turn it, which is the quantity this programme is optimizing. That is the scope its authors claim for it.

Which two fingers you pick changes the answer

KaRMA takes a thumb and an index. real_v1 has three fingers, and this programme’s own result is that the third loaded finger is what keeps the tool: the opposed pair peaks at 0.995 and drops the tool in every rollout with the thumb at 0 N, while inline-plus-thumb settles at 0.884 and keeps it on three loaded fingers. Scoring the same {{PAIR_N}} designs with thumb–index and again with thumb–middle gives Spearman {{PAIR_RHO}} between the two rankings, and a median absolute disagreement of {{PAIR_RATIO}} in log₂ KaRMA-T.

{{CHART_PAIRS}}

Matched on the {{PAIR_N}} designs scored both ways, thumb–index predicts retention at AUC {{PM_TI}} and thumb–middle at {{PM_TM}}; the paired difference is {{PM_DELTA}}, so at this sample size neither pair is demonstrably the better one to hand the metric. Taking the better of the two scores per design reads {{PM_BEST}} and buys nothing. What is established is that a three-finger hand has no single KaRMA score: the number depends on a choice the metric does not make for you, and this hand’s own result is that the third loaded finger is what keeps the tool.

What it is measuring on this family

KaRMA-T is Spearman {{RHO_GEOM}} with the straight-line distance between the two scored mounts, over {{N_DESIGNS}} designs, and {{RHO_LREF}} with L_ref itself. Its ranking is close to “compact hands score higher”, which is what this programme’s funnel already selects: the retained population is more compact than the sampled one in both x_sep (105.2→91.4 mm) and y_sep (110.0→99.8 mm). The {{DELTA}} of AUC that KaRMA adds over that distance is the part of its answer the ruler does not contain.

The pinch it scores is not the grasp this hand uses

KaRMA chooses its own pinch, ranking every feasible seed by a trial search. Over {{N_DESIGNS}} designs it chose a median depth of {{DEPTH_MED}} mm below the mounting plane. The eight deployed plans grasp at 50.5–66.5 mm.

{{CHART_DEPTH}}

The contact model differs too. KaRMA models every finger link as a capsule of one radius — 11.8 mm nominal, 11.37 mm after scaling on this hand — and lets contact land anywhere along it. real_v1’s links are 10.55 mm capsules with a pad sphere at the tip, and the pad is the only surface carrying the high-friction contact parameters. Rebuilding each hand in MuJoCo at KaRMA’s own seed angles, with a sphere of KaRMA’s own radius at KaRMA’s own seed centre, the contact fingers sit a median {{GAP_MED}} mm from the object on the hardware geometry, and {{PAD_PCT}}% of those contacts are nearest at the pad rather than partway up a link.
