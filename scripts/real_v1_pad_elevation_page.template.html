Pad contact elevation and tool retention across eight deployed hands

The two hands that never carry a screwdriver in the chained task are the two whose pads touch it above its equator. A pad-target angle that was hardcoded to zero at every call site moves them below it, and both then complete the chain.

Where the pads meet the shaft

scripts/real_v1_grasp_closure.py · the fitted grip held 0.8 s with the palm stationary

sv1_w6689_b060 (D1) is the best hand on the bench — 6 of 7 trials retained — and it has never carried the tool in the chained task: 0 of {{GRID_N}} rollouts across a grasp grid, 0 of {{D1_LOOP_N}} across the grip loop. It lifts every time. At the lifted seam it holds the tool at 100.8 mm on three pads at 16.71 N; 1.1 s later, with the finger commands unchanged, the tool is on the table 99 mm away.

The reason is where the pads sit on the cylinder. Measuring each pad contact's height above the shaft's own axis, in units of the shaft radius, separates the eight hands without an exception: six contact below the equator and two above it.

{{CLOSURE_FIG}} {{CLOSURE_TBL}}

Fz,pad is the vertical component of the summed pad contact force. On all six carriers it is +0.241 N, which is the screwdriver's weight (0.240 N) to three figures: the pads hold the tool up and nothing else is needed. On D1 it is −3.463 N. Its pads press the tool down with fourteen times its weight, and the tool stays put only because the table is underneath it. Take the table away and it must leave. sv1_w0099_b100 (D8) is the same failure at one tenth the magnitude, with one pad still below the equator.

The rule was already written down

fit_real_v1_pose.tip_targets carries a docstring warning that above the equator the contact normals tilt up and outward, the wedge drives the shaft down out of the pinch, and the normal force it depends on decays as the shaft creeps toward the top of the cylinder. It was written after a grip relaxed 1.75 N to 0.00 N over 0.6 s of holding still. Nothing measured whether a fitted grasp obeyed it.

The servo-load grip loop

real_v1_deploy_envelope._load_step regulates each finger to a servo-load band from measured joint positions and hand FK, without reading object pose or contact force. It is the deployable grip controller and it had never been run at the corrected 2 mm squeeze. It rescues neither failing hand, and it costs four of the six carriers their stand by roughly tripling pad force.

A stale grip depth

Re-fitting depth freely at the chain's squeeze returns the plan's own depth on six of the eight hands, so the depth the chain inherits is not stale. That hypothesis was mine and the measurement refuted it.

The bench-screened turn inside the chain

scripts/real_v1_chain_hands.py --stand air · each plan's own angle_deg, budget and pivot height

Every deployed plan was screened on the fixed-palm bench carry, where the tool stands on a 100 mm post. Until now the chain had only ever been run with angle_deg 0, so the fingers never turned the tool and the screened maneuver had not been tested in the chained task at all. Run at each plan's own setting it stands the tool 0 times in 96.

{{AIR_TBL}}

The turn itself is not the problem. On rv05_manual_b85 the tool goes from 93.0° to 33.2° off vertical — 59.8° of rotation, in the commanded direction. Over the same 1.1 s the pad force falls from 5.60 N to 0.13 N, against a tool that weighs 0.240 N. Rotating the finger anchor through the plan's angle opens the grip. On the bench that is survivable because the post takes the reaction; in free air the tool is released by the same command that turns it.

Sweeping the residual clip and the turn duration at the shipped grasp shows the trade directly, and it is one-dimensional: a larger clip buys rotation and spends grip.

{{BAND_TBL}}

Two cells out of {{BAND_N}} turn the tool through more than 77° and still hold it on loaded pads at the turned seam — sv1_u0060_b75 at 550 steps and clip 0.6 (78.6°, 7.57 N) and rv05_manual_b85 at 250 steps and clip 0.6 (77.6°, 0.85 N). Both are at the largest clip tested, and neither survives the hold that follows.

The pad-ring elevation

scripts/real_v1_chain_hands.py --elevations · 180 chain rollouts, table-stand mode

fit's fourth positional argument is the elevation of the pad-target ring about the shaft: 0° aims the pad centres at the equator, negative aims below it. Every call site in this program passed 0.0. It is now a variable.

At closure it does what the geometry says it should. D1 crosses to three pads below the equator at −24°, and its vertical pad force goes from −3.463 N to +0.241 N — the tool's weight, carried. D8 crosses at −6°. The cost is floor clearance: below about −12° on most hands the fit fails because the pad's underside would pass through the table.

{{ELEV_CLO_TBL}}

In the chain, {{ELEV_STOOD6}} of the 8 hands have an elevation at which they stand the tool 6 times out of 6, and {{ELEV_CARRY_BEST}} of 8 carry it, against {{ELEV_CARRY_SHIP}} of 8 at the shipped 0°. Full chains go from {{ELEV_SHIP_SUM}} of 48 to {{ELEV_BEST_SUM}}. sv1_u0060_b75 is the exception on both counts: it carries at every elevation and stands the tool 5 of 6 at 0° and 0 of 6 below it.

{{ELEV_FIG}} {{ELEV_TBL}}

The optimum is per hand, not global: D3 and D7 are best at the shipped 0°, D4 at −6°, D6 and D8 at −12°, D1 at −24°. Pushing past a hand's own optimum costs it — D7 falls 6/6 to 2/6 by −12°, and D5 stops standing the tool at all below 0°.

Elevation does not touch the other loss. D2 carries 6/6 and stands 6/6 at −6° and completes 0 of 6, because it never re-grips for the gait; D5 is the same at 0°. That seam — releasing a standing tool and closing the ring grasp on it — is untouched by anything on this page.

The turn is still short of the 90° the task wants. Every cell that turns past 59° carries 0 of 4, at either elevation, and every cell that carries 4 of 4 turns 44° or less. The elevation does not lift that ceiling; it decides whether anything is still in the hand underneath it.

What this does not settle

  • The elevation is not in a plan yet. real_v1_deploy_envelope.make_plan calls _grip_from_fit without it and the plan schema has no field for it, so nothing on this page is deployable until that is added and the exported plan re-cleared by scripts/real_v1_trajectory_clearance.py. Falsified if a plan carrying a non-zero elevation fails the clearance gate on the hands that need it.
  • Floor clearance is the binding constraint and it was not measured, only inferred from fit failures. The pad's underside sits 2.0 mm clear of the table at 0°. Measure the actual clearance against elevation per hand and report where it goes to zero, rather than reading it off which fits return None.
  • The turned angle is 42°, not 90°. The best air-mode cell leaves roughly half the reorientation to whatever follows it, and the clip that would buy more turn spends the grip that makes it survivable. Sweep elevation against clip on rv05_manual_b85 alone, finely, at 250 turn steps: −18 and −24 against 0.8/0.9/1.0, scored on the angle turned at turned with pad force ≥ 0.240 N. Falsified if no cell exceeds 60° with a loaded grip.
  • The elevation optimum was found on a five-point grid. D1's is at the edge of it (−24°, the last value that fits), so its true optimum is unmeasured and may be outside the reachable set entirely.
  • The gait re-grip is untouched. D2 and D5 stand the tool and never complete, and D1 stands 6/6 and completes 2/6. That is skill 4, the relay from the carry grasp to the gait ring, and no measurement here addresses it.
  • None of this is hardware. The bench record for these eight hands is 251 runs in docs/experiments/20260902-cb1-log-archive, all at elevation 0. A negative-elevation grasp has never been closed on a real tool.
