Terms and metrics

{{GLOSSARY}}
{{CHAIN}}

Twisting a tool between two fingertips

In a pinch, each fingertip presses on the screwdriver over a small area. Friction at the edge of that area has a lever arm about its centre. The friction forces can cancel as a sideways push while their twisting torques add. At 1 N of normal force per fingertip, the two contact patches resist up to {{HINGE_CAP1}} mN m on the test bed. One mN m is the torque of 1 N acting 1 mm from an axis.

A single point contact has no lever arm about its normal, so it cannot resist this twist. The sphere pad spreads contacts across the area. Each small sphere contributes force and torque through MuJoCo’s usual contact solver. The model can then be used for the same pinch in a CPU planner and in GPU training.

Components of a fingertip contact model

The SR2 hand pinches, turns, rolls and releases a 24.5 g screwdriver between two or three soft fingertips. How the tool moves depends on four contact quantities:

  • the normal force, and the compliance of the fingertip behind it;
  • Coulomb friction in the plane of the contact;
  • the friction torque about the contact normal, which resists twisting;
  • the pressure over the contact area, which sets the friction torque and the centre of pressure.

The friction torque matters most in these tasks. Without it, two fingertips that pinch the tool act as a hinge, and the tool swings down under its own weight. The torque is needed in two maneuvers of Wang, Oh and Pollard (arXiv 2609.25619). In the pinch brake, the hand lowers its pinch force so that the tool swings down slowly. In the wield, a tool that stands in a hole is turned about its axis by repeated pinch, twist and release cycles.

The fingertip is a printed TPU block, 17.0 mm deep, with a 14.8 mm wide palmar face and 2.7 mm fillets. The planned prints have a gyroid infill. Since 13 October the scenes model the block with 1 mm pads. The normal compliance of a printed pad was measured on the UR5 (first part of this page). Its damping, shear compliance and friction have not been measured. The contact model is therefore compared with reference models and analytic limits on the metrics of whole tasks. It is also checked for consistency across time steps and sphere spacings, and its cost is measured. A controller that replans from measured state corrects a consistent model error, as long as the model contains the friction torque and the slip that the task depends on.

The task scenes that use point contact run MuJoCo’s elliptic friction cone at impratio 10, with soft contacts on the fingertips, the tool and the floor (solref 0.006, solimp 0.97–0.995).

Contact mechanics of a soft fingertip

1Friction torque of a contact patch

Pressure is force divided by area. Let \(p\) be the pressure at one place on the contact patch \(A\), in N/mm². A small piece of area \(dA\) presses with normal force \(p\,dA\), in N. Its greatest friction force is \(\mu p\,dA\), where \(\mu\) is the friction coefficient.

Let \(r\) be the distance of that piece from the centre of the patch, in mm. During a twist, its friction acts along a circle around the centre. The force is perpendicular to its lever, so its torque is \(r\mu p\,dA\), in mN m. For example, put two 0.5 N pieces 1 mm on opposite sides of the centre and set \(\mu=1\). Their sideways friction forces cancel, while their torques add to 1 mN m.

Top view of the two-piece example. The friction forces point in opposite directions. Each has a 1 mm lever about the centre, so both torques turn the tool the same way.

Adding the pieces gives the total normal force \(F\) and the largest torque \(T\) before the whole patch spins. The integral sign means this sum as the pieces become smaller:

\[F=\int_A p\,dA,\qquad T=\mu\int_A p\,r\,dA=\mu F\,\bar r,\qquad \bar r=\frac{\int_A p\,r\,dA}{\int_A p\,dA}.\](1)

The friction arm \(\bar r\) is the average lever length, with each place weighted by its normal force. Pressure near the edge gives a longer arm than the same force near the centre. Opposite sides add torque in the same direction during a twist.

For a circular patch of radius \(a\) with uniform pressure, add the patch as thin rings. A ring at distance \(r\) has circumference \(2\pi r\), so its area is \(2\pi r\,dr\). Multiplying by \(p\) gives its normal force; multiplying again by \(\mu r\) gives its torque:

\[F=\int_0^a p\,2\pi r\,dr=p\,\pi a^2,\qquad T=\mu\int_0^a p\,r\,2\pi r\,dr=\mu p\,\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}.\]

For the force integral, adding \(r\,dr\) from 0 to \(a\) gives \(a^2/2\). The extra lever \(r\) in the torque integral changes that sum to \(a^3/3\). The factors of \(p\), \(\mu\) and \(2\pi\) stay fixed.

Dividing the torque by \(\mu F\) gives \(\bar r=2a/3\). The average lever is more than half the radius because the outer rings have more area. With \(u=r/a\), the distance as a fraction of the radius, other pressure shapes give these friction arms:

  • \(a/2\) under a cone of pressure, \(1-u\);
  • \(8a/15=0.533\,a\) under the paraboloid \(1-u^2\) of a Winkler foundation (step 2);
  • \(3\pi a/16=0.589\,a\) under the Hertz profile \(\sqrt{1-u^2}\) (step 3);
  • \(2a/3\) under a uniform pressure;
  • \(a\) when all the pressure lies in a ring at the rim.

A point contact acts at \(r=0\), so it resists no twist. Two point contacts make a hinge about the line through them. The screwdriver’s centre of mass is \(d\) = {{HINGE_D}} mm from that line when the tool is horizontal. Its weight \(mg\) pulls downward, giving a torque of \(mgd\) = {{HINGE_MGD}} mN m. At an angle \(\varphi\) below horizontal, the perpendicular lever of the weight is \(d\cos\varphi\).

Each pad can resist up to \(\mu F\bar r(F)\) before spinning. Two pads keep the tool still when their combined torque is at least the torque from gravity. A still tool has zero net torque: gravity tries to turn it down, and friction can oppose that turn.

\[2\mu F\,\bar r(F)\ \ge\ mgd\cos\varphi .\]

At 1 N per pad the arm on the screwdriver is {{HINGE_ARM1}} mm (step 2), so the two pads resist up to {{HINGE_CAP1}} mN m with \(\mu=1\). The tool then comes to rest at \(\varphi\) = {{HINGE_PHI1}}°. It stays horizontal when each pad presses with {{HINGE_FH}} N or more. The braked swing of task 5 on the test bed ends at {{HINGE_N1}} N. There the pads still resist {{HINGE_CAPN1}} mN m, and the tool rests at {{HINGE_PHIN1}}°. Point contacts hold the tool only hanging straight down, at 90°.

2Winkler foundation and the hydroelastic pressure field

A layer of independent springs, the Winkler foundation, gives a friction arm that grows as the fourth root of the force. Drake’s hydroelastic contact is such a layer.

Imagine a bed of separate vertical springs under the fingertip surface. Each spring pushes back in proportion to its own compression \(\delta\). The pressure is \(p=k_w\delta\), where \(k_w=E/h\) is the stiffness per unit area of a layer with modulus \(E\) and thickness \(h\). With lengths in mm, \(E\) is in MPa (N/mm²) and \(k_w\) is in N/mm³. Neighbouring springs do not share load in this model. Drake’s hydroelastic pressure field (Elandt et al., 2019) follows this rule. For a fingertip sphere of radius \(R\), its field uses \(h=R\).

Press the sphere into a flat tool by \(\delta_0\) at its centre. Farther from the centre, the sphere curves away from the tool. Pythagoras gives a height of \(R-\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\) at distance \(r\) from the centre. Close to the centre, that height is about \(r^2/(2R)\). That spring compresses by \(\delta_0-r^2/(2R)\), if this value is positive. At the patch edge the compression reaches zero. Solving \(\delta_0-a^2/(2R)=0\) gives the patch radius \(a=\sqrt{2R\delta_0}\).

The pressure at each distance is \(k_w\) times that local compression. Each thin ring has area \(2\pi r\,dr\), as in step 1. Adding the forces of the rings gives:

\[F=2\pi k_w\int_0^a\Bigl(\delta_0-\frac{r^2}{2R}\Bigr)r\,dr =2\pi k_w\Bigl(\frac{\delta_0a^2}{2}-\frac{a^4}{8R}\Bigr) =\pi k_wR\,\delta_0^2 .\](2)

The first equality adds the force from each ring. The second evaluates the integral. Substituting \(a^2=2R\delta_0\) then gives the final expression for \(F\).

The force grows as \(\delta_0^2\) because two things grow in proportion to \(\delta_0\): the patch area \(2\pi R\delta_0\) and the mean pressure \(k_w\delta_0/2\). The pressure \(k_w\delta_0(1-r^2/a^2)\) is the paraboloid of step 1, so \(\bar r=8a/15\). Equation (2) gives \(a^4=4R^2\delta_0^2=4RF/(\pi k_w)\), and substituting it writes the arm as a power of the force:

\[\bar r_W=\frac{8}{15}\Bigl(\frac{4R}{\pi k_w}\Bigr)^{1/4}F^{1/4}=c\,F^{1/4},\qquad T=\mu c\,F^{5/4}.\](3)

The arm grows as the fourth root of the force, and the friction torque as its 5/4 power.

The real_v1 fingertip sphere has \(k_w=E/R\) = {{KW}} N/mm³ at Drake’s default \(E\) = 10 MPa. A force of 1 N sinks it {{BED_D0_UM}} µm into a flat face. The patch then has a radius of {{BED_A_MM}} mm and a peak pressure of {{BED_P0}} MPa. The contact stiffness \(dF/d\delta_0=2\pi k_wR\delta_0\) is {{BED_KC}} N/mm, and \(c={{C_FLAT}}\ \mathrm{mm\,N^{-1/4}}\).

On the 12.5 mm screwdriver the patch is narrower across the tool’s axis. Integrating the same field over the overlap of the sphere and the cylinder (scripts/hydroelastic_arm_integral.py) gives arms of 0.820, 0.974 and 1.281 mm at 0.5, 1 and 3 N. That is \(c=0.974\ \mathrm{mm\,N^{-1/4}}\), with the exponent 1/4 to within 0.1 %. This integral is the reference for the twist task. Drake and the sphere pads are two discretizations of it. The springs are independent, so the pressure is zero outside the overlap.

3Hertz contact

A solid elastic fingertip spreads load sideways, and its friction arm grows as \(F^{1/3}\), faster than the foundation’s \(F^{1/4}\). The two laws can be told apart by how the twist torque grows with force.

A homogeneous elastic body passes load sideways through shear. Its surface therefore deflects outside the loaded region, and the pressure takes the Hertz form. For a sphere on a rigid flat, with \(E^\ast=E/(1-\nu^2)\) and \(\nu\) Poisson’s ratio,

\[a^3=\frac{3FR}{4E^\ast},\qquad p(r)=\frac{3F}{2\pi a^2}\sqrt{1-\frac{r^2}{a^2}},\qquad \bar r_H=\frac{3\pi}{16}\,a\;\propto\;F^{1/3}.\](4)

At the same approach an elastic solid touches over a smaller patch than the foundation: \(a^2=R\delta\) against \(2R\delta\). Its mean pressure also grows with the strain \(a/R\). Together these give \(F\propto\delta^{3/2}\) and \(a\propto F^{1/3}\).

Drake’s 10 MPa is a parameter of its pressure field, \(k_wR\). Read as a Young’s modulus with \(\nu=0.45\) (\(E^\ast\) = {{HZ_ESTAR}} MPa), it gives \(a\) = {{HZ_A}} mm and \(\bar r\) = {{HZ_RBAR}} mm at 1 N by (4). That is half the foundation’s {{C_FLAT}} mm. Between 0.5 and 3 N the arm grows

\[\frac{\bar r(3\,\mathrm{N})}{\bar r(0.5\,\mathrm{N})}=6^{1/4}=1.565\ \text{(foundation)},\qquad 6^{1/3}=1.817\ \text{(Hertz)}.\](5)

The modulus enters only the prefactor \(c\) of \(\bar r=c\,F^{\gamma}\), with \(\gamma\) = 1/4 on a foundation and 1/3 under Hertz. The ratio \(\bar r(F_2)/\bar r(F_1)=(F_2/F_1)^{\gamma}\) therefore does not depend on the modulus, and a bench can test the law without knowing it. A bench measures the torque \(T=\mu F\bar r\propto F^{1+\gamma}\). From 0.5 to 3 N the torque grows {{T_RATIO_BED}} times on a foundation and {{T_RATIO_HZ}} times under Hertz. Dividing the spin torque by the pull force at slip under the same pinch, \(\bar r=T/F_\text{slip}\) for two pads, removes the friction coefficient.

Xydas and Kao measured exponents from 0 to 1/3 on soft fingertips. A tip with \(\bar r\propto F^{0.15}\), inside that range, gives a ratio of {{RATIO_015}}, which neither law fits. The test holds for a spherical tip. On the printed TPU block the screwdriver presses a line across a flat face. In finite-element models of the planned prints the arm then grows only {{FE_ARM_RATIO}} times from 0.5 to 3 N.

4Lateral coupling: the Pasternak foundation

A Pasternak foundation adds a shear layer that couples neighbouring springs:

\[p=k_w\,\delta-G\,\nabla^2\delta,\qquad \ell=\sqrt{G/k_w}.\](6)

Load then spreads a coupling length \(\ell\) beyond the overlap. A Winkler foundation is enough when \(\ell\) is much smaller than the patch radius \(a\). A coupled foundation is needed when \(\ell\) is close to \(a\), and a shell or modal model when \(\ell\) is much larger. {{CSLC_MECH}} The asides page describes how \(\ell\) would be measured on the printed tip ({{ASIDES_REF}}).

Sphere-packed pads in MuJoCo

5Sphere discretization of the foundation

A pad of small spheres reproduces the Winkler foundation when each sphere is given the foundation’s stiffness times its share of the area. Pressed on a flat with 1 N, the 1 mm pad’s arm is {{FLAT_ARM}} mm, against the foundation’s {{FLAT_BED}} mm.

The pad replaces the pressure field with \(n\) spheres of radius \(r_s\). The spheres sit inside the fingertip, and their outer surfaces trace its shape. Each sphere is an ordinary MuJoCo contact with condim 3: a normal force and friction in two directions. The pad’s force and torque are sums over the spheres that touch the tool:

\[F\approx\sum_i N_i,\qquad T\approx\sum_i r_i\,f_{t,i},\qquad \lVert f_{t,i}\rVert\le\mu N_i .\](7)

Each sphere is a spring with the foundation’s stiffness \(E/R\) times its share \(A_s\) of the pad’s area. One correction is made for the sphere radius. A sphere touches the tool at the point under its centre, and the centres lie on a sphere of radius \(R-r_s\). Seen from the tool, the lattice is therefore shrunk by \((R-r_s)/R\) in each direction. Its contact points are \((R/(R-r_s))^2\) = {{PAD_DENS}} times as dense as on the fingertip surface, and the stiffness corrects for this:

\[k_s=\frac{E}{R}\,A_s\Bigl(\frac{R-r_s}{R}\Bigr)^2 .\](8)

The same geometry makes the pad a foundation of curvature radius \(R-r_s\), slightly smaller than \(R\). At equal force its arm is \(((R-r_s)/R)^{1/4}\) = {{PAD_ARMFAC}} times the foundation’s.

On the test bed the 1 mm pad covers a {{BED_CAP}}° cap of the fingertip sphere with \(n\) = {{BED_N}} spheres of \(k_s\) = {{BED_KS}} N/m. The chain’s thumb pad, shown in Figures 5 and 6, covers a {{CH_CAP}}° cap with {{CH_N}} spheres of {{CH_KS}} N/m. Pressed on a flat with 1 N, {{FLAT_N}} of the test bed pad’s spheres touch. Their arm is {{FLAT_ARM}} mm, against {{FLAT_BED}} mm for the foundation and {{FLAT_PRED}} mm for a foundation of curvature \(R-r_s\). Halving the spacing gives four times as many spheres, each a quarter as stiff, so the stiffness per unit area stays \(k_w\). Figure 5 shows the sphere forces sampling the field’s pressure at 3 N. Figure 6 shows the patches at 3 and 0.5 N.

The spacing must stay small compared with the patch radius, which is {{PATCH_A}} mm at 0.5–3 N on a flat. A 2 mm pad is too coarse at 0.5 N, where it touches with {{PL2_N}} spheres. Tilting its lattice by 0–8° before pressing then moves its arm {{PL2}} the foundation’s. Under the same tilts the 1 mm pad’s arm moves {{PL1}}, and the 0.5 mm pad’s {{PL05}}. In the twist on the test bed at 0.5 N, the 2 mm pad’s arm is {{P2_ONSET_DEV}} % below the law at spin onset and {{P2_KIN_DEV}} % below it while spinning.

6Static stiffness of a MuJoCo soft contact

At rest each MuJoCo contact row acts as a spring whose stiffness is set by three numbers: the time constant of solref, the impedance of solimp and the contact’s inverse inertia. A pad at rest is therefore a discrete Winkler foundation.

A MuJoCo contact contributes rows to the solver: one for the normal direction and one or more for friction. Each step, MuJoCo solves one linear system for the forces \(f\) of all the rows. For each row it sets a reference acceleration \(a_\text{ref}\), which pulls the penetration back to zero like a damped spring. A regularizer \(R\) makes the contact soft:

\[\begin{aligned} (A+R)\,f&=a_\text{ref}-a_0,\qquad A=J M^{-1}J^\top,\qquad R=\operatorname{diag}\Bigl(\frac{1-d}{d}\,\hat\Lambda_i\Bigr),\\ a_\text{ref}&=-b\,v-k\,r,\qquad b=\frac{2}{d_\text{max}t_c},\qquad k=\frac{d}{d_\text{max}^2t_c^2}. \end{aligned}\](9)

Here \(a_0\) is the rows’ acceleration without contact, and \(r\) and \(v\) are the penetration and its rate. The time constant \(t_c\) is the first value of solref, and the impedance \(d\) comes from solimp. \(\hat\Lambda_i\) is MuJoCo’s estimate of the inverse inertia of row \(i\): the sum of the two bodies’ body_invweight0. The Delassus matrix \(A\) couples every pair of rows on a shared body. \(R\) is diagonal, because each \(R_i\) uses only its own \(\hat\Lambda_i\).

Write \(a_1=a_0+Af\) for the acceleration with contact. Equation (9) then splits into one equation per row, for any \(A\) and any force inside its cone:

\[R_if_i=a_{\text{ref},i}-a_{1,i}.\]

The rows affect each other only through \(a_1\). At rest \(a_1=v=0\) for every row, so \(R_if_i=-k\,r_i\). With a constant impedance \(d=d_\text{max}=d_0\), the stiffness term is \(k=1/(d_0t_c^2)\) and the regularizer is \(R_i=(1-d_0)\hat\Lambda_i/d_0\). Dividing one by the other gives the force of one sphere:

\[f_i=k_s\,\lvert r_i\rvert,\qquad k_s=\frac{1}{t_c^2\,(1-d_0)\,\hat\Lambda_i},\qquad c_s=2t_c\,k_s .\](10)

Here \(k_s=k/R_i\) is the row’s stiffness. \(c_s=b/R_i\) is its damping coefficient in N s/m, the force per unit penetration rate when \(a_1=0\). Each sphere is a spring of stiffness \(k_s\), whatever its neighbours do, so at rest the pad is a discrete Winkler foundation. In Codex’s static audit the force per overlap equalled (10) to floating-point precision (docs/experiments/20261004-codex/20261004-distributed_contact.html).

While the pad moves, its spheres do interact. When the pad slides along the normal against a plate, all its spheres measure the same motion. Every entry of \(A\) is then \(\hat\Lambda\), and \(A\) has the eigenvalue \(n\hat\Lambda\) along the common motion. Pressing one sphere harder accelerates the pad and unloads the others. At rest \(a_1=0\) removes that coupling.

Away from rest a single row with \(A=\hat\Lambda\) has \(a_1=d_0a_\text{ref}+(1-d_0)a_0\), and its penetration obeys

\[\ddot r+\frac{2}{t_c}\,\dot r+\frac{r}{t_c^2}=(1-d_0)\,a_0 .\]

This is a critically damped motion with time constant \(t_c\), for any \(d_0\) and \(\hat\Lambda\). A sphere pushed from rest returns as \((1+t/t_c)\,e^{-t/t_c}\). It is within 5 % of rest after \({{REL_X}}\,t_c\), which is {{REL_MS}} ms at \(t_c\) = {{TC_MS}} ms. Under slow loading the ratio of the row’s damping to its stiffness, \(c_s/k_s=2t_c\) in (10), is {{TC2_MS}} ms. Drake’s corresponding dissipation time is 10 ms.

Task 6 on the test bed varies \(t_c\) at 1 N, over {{ONSET_TCS}}. The slip force gives an effective \(\mu\) of {{MU_TC}}. The arm while spinning at 1 rad/s is {{KIN_TC}} mm. The arm at spin onset grows with \(t_c\), as {{ONSET_TC}} mm, through the friction dampers of step 8.

7Inverse-inertia calibration

The sphere stiffness in (10) depends on the masses of the pad and the tool through \(\hat\Lambda\). The impedance \(d_0\) is therefore computed from the compiled model for each pad and object, and the arm then no longer depends on the tool’s mass (Figure 7).

The value \(k\) of solref is an acceleration per metre, in s\(^{-2}\). Dividing it by \(R_i\), in kg\(^{-1}\), turns it into a force per metre. That is how the bodies’ masses enter (10). Solved for the impedance,

\[d_0=1-\frac{1}{k_s\hat\Lambda t_c^2},\qquad 0(k_s\hat\Lambda)^{-1/2}.\]

A valid impedance lies between 0 and 1. At a given \(t_c\) the softest spring MuJoCo can give is therefore \(1/(\hat\Lambda t_c^2)\), reached as \(d_0\to0\). A softer sphere is possible only with a longer time constant.

On the railed pads of the test bed, \(\hat\Lambda\) = {{BED_WP}} + {{W_TOOL}} = {{BED_LAM}} kg\(^{-1}\): the inverse weights of a {{BED_PADG}} g pad and the 24.5 g tool. For the 1 mm pad’s spheres of {{BED_KS}} N/m, \(t_c\) must then exceed {{BED_TCMIN}} ms, and \(d_0\) is {{BED_D0}} at {{TC_MS}} ms. For the 0.5 mm pad’s spheres of {{BED_KS05}} N/m, \(t_c\) must exceed {{BED_TCMIN05}} ms. On the chain’s thumb, \(\hat\Lambda\) = {{TH_WP}} + {{W_TOOL}} = {{TH_LAM}} kg\(^{-1}\). For its 1 mm and 0.5 mm pads, \(t_c\) must exceed {{TH_TCMIN}} and {{TH_TCMIN05}} ms, and its 1 mm pad’s \(d_0\) is {{TH_D0}} at {{TC_MS}} ms.

Because \(\hat\Lambda\) includes the tool’s inverse weight, a tool of the wrong mass changes every sphere’s stiffness. The arm depends on the stiffness only through the patch radius:

\[\bar r\propto a\propto\Bigl(\frac{F}{k_s}\Bigr)^{1/4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta\bar r}{\bar r}\approx-\frac14\,\frac{\Delta k_s}{k_s}.\](11)

Let \(w_p\) be the pad’s inverse weight and \(m_0\) the nominal tool mass. With \(d_0\) fixed, a tool \(s\) times heavier scales every sphere’s stiffness by

\[\frac{k_s(s\,m_0)}{k_s(m_0)}=\frac{w_p+1/m_0}{w_p+1/(s\,m_0)}.\]

For a tool four times heavier the factor is {{MASSF_BED}} on the test bed and {{MASSF_TH}} on the thumb. The arm then shortens by {{ARMF_BED}} and {{ARMF_TH}} %. A wrong tool mass matters most where the pad is heavy compared with the tool. {{MASS_TEXT}}

8Creep under MuJoCo’s soft friction

Below the friction limit MuJoCo’s soft friction acts as a damper. A pad under a steady sideways load therefore slides slowly. This sliding, called creep, is proportional to the load and inversely proportional to impratio.

Rigid Coulomb friction gives no creep. A sphere’s friction rows enter (9) with the reference acceleration \(a_\text{ref}=-b\,v\) and no position term. Their regularizer is \(R_t=R_n/\kappa\), where \(\kappa\) is impratio. At steady sliding \(a_1=0\), so each friction row obeys \(R_tf_t=-b\,v\). With \(b=2/(d_0t_c)\) and (10), the friction row is a damper \(c_t\), in N s/m, \(\kappa\) times the normal row’s coefficient \(c_s\):

\[f_t=-\frac{b}{R_t}\,v=-\frac{2\kappa}{(1-d_0)\,\hat\Lambda\,t_c}\,v=-c_t\,v,\qquad c_t=\kappa\,c_s=2\kappa\,k_s\,t_c .\]

Once \(d_0\) is set for \(k_s\) (step 7), \((1-d_0)\hat\Lambda=1/(k_st_c^2)\), so neither \(d_0\) nor \(\hat\Lambda\) remains in \(c_t\). All touching spheres slide together. When \(n\) spheres share a sideways load \(F_t\) equally, they creep at

\[v_\text{creep}=\frac{F_t}{n\,c_t}=\frac{F_t}{2\kappa\,n\,k_s\,t_c}.\](12)

Under rigid Coulomb friction, how a load below slip is shared between the spheres is undetermined. MuJoCo’s dampers share it equally. The outer spheres press with the smallest normal forces \(N_i\), so they reach their limits \(\mu N_i\) first, and the inner ones take the rest:

\[F_t=\sum_i\min\bigl(c_t\,v,\ \mu N_i\bigr).\]

9Step-size bound for a pad

A pad stays stable in MuJoCo’s integrator while the time step is below \(2t_c(\sqrt{1+q}-1)\), where \(q\) falls from 1 to \(d_0\) as more spheres touch. For the 1 mm pads at \(t_c\) = {{TC_MS}} ms the bound is {{SB_ONE}} ms when one sphere touches and {{SB_LIM}} ms when many do.

Away from rest the rows of one pad move together, and their common motion can become unstable at a long step. For \(n\) rows of equal \(\hat\Lambda\) on one body, \(A\) has the eigenvalue \(n\hat\Lambda\) along that motion. In (9) the motion then has an effective impedance \(d'\), a damping rate \(\beta\) in s\(^{-1}\) and a spring \(\omega^2\):

\[d'=\frac{n\,d_0}{n\,d_0+1-d_0},\qquad \beta=d'\,b=\frac{2}{q\,t_c},\qquad \omega^2=d'\,k=\frac{1}{q\,t_c^2},\qquad q=\frac{d_0}{d'}=d_0+\frac{1-d_0}{n}.\](13)

On a body that also turns, the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_\text{max}\) of \(A\) takes the place of \(n\hat\Lambda\), and \(q=d_0+(1-d_0)\hat\Lambda/\lambda_\text{max}\). MuJoCo evaluates \(a_\text{ref}=-b\,v-k\,r\) at the state at the start of the step, so the spring is as explicit as the damper. For \(\ddot x=-\beta\,\dot x-\omega^2x\), MuJoCo updates the velocity and then the position with the new velocity. Over a step \(\Delta t\) this gives the step matrix

\[\begin{bmatrix}x\\ v\end{bmatrix}_{j+1}=\begin{bmatrix}1-\Delta t^2\omega^2 & \Delta t\,(1-\Delta t\,\beta)\\ -\Delta t\,\omega^2 & 1-\Delta t\,\beta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\ v\end{bmatrix}_j .\]

The motion stays stable while the matrix’s eigenvalues lie inside the unit circle. That holds when \(\Delta t\,\beta<2\) and \(\Delta t^2\omega^2+2\Delta t\,\beta<4\). With (13) the second condition reads \((\Delta t/t_c)^2+4\Delta t/t_c<4q\), which gives the bound:

\[\Delta t<2t_c\bigl(\sqrt{1+q}-1\bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;\begin{cases}2(\sqrt2-1)\,t_c, & n=1,\\ 2t_c\bigl(\sqrt{1+d_0}-1\bigr), & n\to\infty.\end{cases}\](14)

With the damper condition alone, \(\Delta twxie_workspace/contact, script tutorial/09_fingertip_contact.py, row d23_step_threshold). It bisects the spectral radius of MuJoCo’s one-step map for this pad on an undamped slide. The radius crosses 1 at the bound to within \(10^{-4}t_c\), at 24 pairs of \(t_c\) and \(d_0\). For the 1 mm pads of these pages (\(t_c\) = {{TC_MS}} ms, \(d_0\) = {{SB_D0S}}) the bound falls from {{SB_ONE}} ms for one touching sphere to {{SB_LIM}} ms for many.

In Codex’s 50 Hz sweep the pads gave the same task results at steps from 50 µs to 10 ms (Figure 10). Codex’s earlier per-contact mapping set \(d_0=10^{-4}\) to impose each sphere’s spring and damper exactly. Then \(q\approx1/n\), and the damping rate is \(\beta\approx nc_s\hat\Lambda\), with \(c_s\) = 24.5 N s/m per sphere. Stability then holds only for \(\Delta t<2/(nc_s\hat\Lambda)\), which is 60–90 µs for 20–30 spheres. Those runs diverged at every step from 0.5 to 10 ms and completed only at 50 µs. MuJoCo’s clamp \(t_c\ge2\Delta t\) acts on each row alone, so it does not limit this collective motion.

10condim 4 with a rescheduled torsional coefficient

MuJoCo’s condim 4 gives one contact point a torsional friction coefficient. Reset every step from the last normal force, that coefficient reproduces the torque law (3) with one contact per pad.

condim 4 keeps one contact per pad and adds the torsional coefficient \(\mu_t\). The tangential force and the torque share one elliptic cone, \((f_1^2+f_2^2)/\mu^2+\tau^2/\mu_t^2\le N^2\). To match (3), \(\mu_t\) must equal \(\mu\bar r(N)\), which changes with the load. The plant therefore rewrites \(\mu_t\) before every step from the last normal force:

\[\mu_t[j]=\mu\,c\,N[j-1]^{1/4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau_\text{max}[j]\approx\mu\,c\,N^{5/4}.\](15)

On the test bed condim 4 takes \(c\) and the exponent from a fit to Drake’s torque on this rig (\(c=0.996\ \mathrm{mm\,N^{-1/4}}\), exponent 0.2498). It reproduces Drake’s torque while the tool spins. At spin onset its torque lies above the law (the twist task below). It costs one coefficient write per contact per step. With a single contact point its centre of pressure cannot move, and under a pure pull or roll it behaves as condim 3. The chain page derives the brake that uses it ({{CHAIN_PATH}}, steps 3 and 5).

Drake and the spheres integrate the same pressure law, which is local and has no state. With the friction rows stiffened by impratio 1000, the sprung skin adds the shear compliance before slip, and the bristle model adds its hysteresis at {{BRISTLE_X}} times the batched cost. At the policy trainer’s impratio of 10 the creep of the friction rows is larger than the deflection of the skin’s springs. Neither model spreads load sideways as an elastic tip does. On a spherical tip both give a contact radius that grows as \(N^{1/4}\), and measured soft fingertips give exponents from 0 to 1/3, depending on the material (Xydas and Kao). The spheres add the creep of MuJoCo’s soft friction rows, and they miss surface features finer than their 1 mm spacing.
