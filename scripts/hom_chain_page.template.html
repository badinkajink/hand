Problem and hypothesis

a pinch brake needs a contact area, and MuJoCo resolves contact at points

The paper’s load step turns the thumb–index pinch into a brake. The tool hangs between the two pads and turns about the line through them. Only friction spread over each contact area resists that turn: T = μ ∫p ρ dA, with p the contact pressure and ρ the distance from the contact normal. A point contact has no area, so it transmits no torque about its normal. With point contact the pinched tool is a free hinge, in MuJoCo and in Drake alike; the point-contact films below drop it into a hang as soon as it leaves the posts. The paper uses Drake’s hydroelastic contact for this reason. The question here is whether MuJoCo can carry the same controller without it.

Drake’s compliant-hydroelastic fingertip carries a pressure field p = E·d/R, with d the depth below its surface. Drake evaluates the field on the rigid tool surface wherever the two overlap (Figure 1, left). For a sphere on a plane the integrals give the elastic-foundation laws F = πEδ₀² and T = μF·(8/15)a with a = √(2Rδ₀), so the friction arm T/μF grows as F1/4. Take the real_v1 fingertip (R = 10.55 mm) on the screwdriver (r = 12.5 mm) at Drake’s default E = 10 MPa. At 3 N the integrated field covers {{HI_AREA}} mm² and gives an arm of {{HI_ARM}} mm, against {{HI_RIG}} mm measured in Drake on the two-pad rig.

The hypothesis is that springs can replace the field. MuJoCo resolves each overlapping pair of geoms as one soft contact. At steady state that contact acts as a linear spring of stiffness 1/(tc²(1−d0)Λ), for solref (tc, 1) and a constant solimp d0; Λ is MuJoCo’s inverse-inertia estimate for the contact. Cover the fingertip with small spheres and give each the foundation’s stiffness over its share of the pad, k = (E/R)·As·((R−rs)/R)². The spring forces then sample ∫p dA and their moments sample ∫p ρ dA (Figure 1, right). Contact area, centre of pressure and pinch torque come out of ordinary point contacts, with no change to MuJoCo. The spacing has to be fine compared with the contact patch (Figure 2).

  • At 3 N: every spacing gives the field’s arm within {{HI_DEV_MAX}} %.
  • At 0.5 N, the force at which the brake sets the end of the swing: the 2 mm pad touches with only {{LO_N2}} spheres and its arm is {{LO_DEV2}} % long. The 1 and 0.5 mm pads stay within {{LO_DEV_FINE}} %.

A second, cheaper surrogate keeps a single contact point and supplies the torque directly: condim 4, with a torsional coefficient μt = μ·r̄(N) recomputed every step from the measured law. Earlier results:

  • Two-pad rig: the rescheduled condim 4 matched Drake’s torque within 1 %; the 1 and 0.5 mm pads matched it within 7 and 3 %.
  • Hand load step: all three agreed with Drake within 3–9 %.

This page asks whether the surrogates carry a controller through a whole task with one code base driving Drake and MuJoCo, and what each costs. Figure 3 follows the thumb contact through the nominal chain in five of the models.

Controller and chain

the paper’s generalized contact frames and least squares, a pad force through the servos, a closed-loop brake

Each fingertip gets a generalized contact frame on the tool. Its origin is at the witness points, x runs along the tool’s signed-distance gradient, and y is the finger’s flexion axis projected onto the tangent plane. The relative contact twist (spin, two roll, separation and two slide components; paper Eq. 1) is linear in the finger’s three joint rates. The joint-rate reference is the bounded least squares of Eq. 2, with damping and healthy-pose terms, integrated into the servo targets at 100 Hz. For a sphere against a cylinder the frames are exact in closed form and stay smooth through contact, so the paper’s geodesic filter on the hydroelastic centre of pressure is left out. The controller runs on a kinematic MuJoCo copy of the scene, so the same code drives the MuJoCo and the Drake plant.

The chain has seven steps.

  • Pick: the palm descends with thumb and index 8 mm open. They close along their contact normals (vsep = −15 mm/s, slide held at zero) and switch to force at contact. Pad force F enters as q = qtouch + JT(−F n)/kp + τg/kp, where τg is the fingers’ own weight.
  • Lift: the palm rises 90 mm at F = 4 N.
  • Brake: the swing about the pinch axis runs open loop (4 → 0.5 N over 3 s) or closed loop. The closed-loop brake tracks a 1.8 s minimum-jerk profile to 90°. It inverts Iφ̈ = mgd cosφ − τf(N) through the rig’s brake law, with nominal mass and μ. Its force floor starts at twice the slip limit W/2μ, falls while the tool stalls off vertical, and rises 50 % when the tool slides along its axis. It accepts the swing within 2.5°.
  • Hold firm: F rises to 3 N.
  • Transport and aim: the palm carries the tool’s lower end, aimed from its measured pose, 8 mm over the hole and corrects once.
  • Insert: the palm descends 30 mm at 15 mm/s.

The pinch line is the thumb–index mount line turned 45° about the palm normal, 50 mm below the mounts. There both fingers keep 0.55 rad of joint range from 4 mm squeeze to 12 mm open. The 2026-10-01 brake study’s line through the mount midpoint at 45 mm puts the index on its mcp and pip limits at contact, and the pinch cannot open there.

Experiment 1: contact-free tracking

the index steps each relative-velocity component at 40 °/s or 20 mm/s while the other five are held at zero

{{EXP1_TABLE}}

With all six components weighted equally, as in the paper’s protocol, a three-joint finger cannot follow a pure step. It translates its tip only by turning it, so the least squares trades each commanded component against the five held at zero. The gain is the median ratio of achieved to commanded on the stepped component. {{EXP1_PROSE}} The paper reports RMSE for five hands in a figure without stating the values in its text.

Simulation cost

the nominal chain under the closed-loop brake, one core, {{REP_RANGE}} repeats per model

Physics is the time spent inside the simulator’s step, at a 1 ms step. The controller is the same code in every model: state read-out, mirror kinematics, contact frames, least squares and brake. It costs {{CTRL_MJ}} ms per simulated second against MuJoCo and {{CTRL_DK}} ms against Drake, whose state read-out is slower. Repeats agree within {{REP_SPREAD}} %.

  • Against condim 4: the 1 mm sphere pad costs {{X_S1_P4S}}× as much.
  • Against Drake hydroelastic at its default 1 mm mesh: the 1 mm pad is {{X_DHY_S1}}× cheaper and the 0.5 mm pad {{X_DHY_S05}}× cheaper.
  • What scales the cost: a sphere pad’s cost follows its spheres in contact, {{NC_S1}} in the hold for the 1 mm pad and {{NC_S05}} for the 0.5 mm pad. Drake’s follows its contact-surface faces, {{NC_DHY}} in the hold at 1 mm, and roughly doubles each time the mesh halves.
  • Stage: the stages at full pinch force (lift at 4 N; hold, transport and insert at 3 N) cost two to three times the brake. In the brake the force falls below 1 N and the patch shrinks.
