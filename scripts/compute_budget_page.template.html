Where the wall clock goes

median over {{N_LOGS}} logs, iteration 0 excluded

Collection takes {{COLLECT_PCT}}% of an iteration and the PPO update takes {{LEARN_PCT}}%; the worst learning share in the whole population is {{LEARN_MAX}}%. Iteration time minus collection minus learning leaves {{UNACC}}% unaccounted, so there is no hidden third term. Every learning-side knob — epochs, minibatch count, learning rate, network width — divides a budget of one and a third percent.

Batch width does not help either, and the reason is visible in the per-env-step cost. One iteration simulates num_envs × 24 env-steps, so dividing collection time by that gives a cost per simulated step that is free of batch size. On the workstation it is {{US1}} µs at 1024 envs, {{US2}} µs at 2048 and {{US3}} µs at 3072 — a spread of 0.01% across a threefold change in batch. The GH200 behaves the same way at its own level, {{GH_US_ASYM}} µs from 8192 to 16384.

{{CHART_COST}}

A workload whose per-item cost is invariant to batch width is not throughput-limited. It is limited by the depth of the dependency chain inside one step: MuJoCo's solver runs iterations × ls_iterations sequential passes, and with 15 joints and a handful of contacts none of those passes is wide enough to fill the device. That matches what the card reports while training, 43–46% utilisation with CUDA graph capture already enabled, and it explains why the two levers people reach for first are both spent: the solver already ships at iterations=10, ls_iterations=20 (set in env_build.py, overriding whatever the scene XML says), and adding environments widens kernels that were never the constraint.

Why the GH200 loses on a workload it should win

clock ratio {{CLK_RATIO}}× against measured cost ratio {{COST_RATIO}}×

A latency-bound workload spends its time waiting on a chain of dependent operations, so its wall clock scales with clock period rather than with FLOPs or bandwidth. That predicts the deficit directly. The workstation's SM clock reads {{CLK_LOC}} MHz under load (nvidia-smi --query-gpu=clocks.sm, 2026-09-08, ceiling 3,120); the GH200's Hopper die boosts to a specified {{CLK_GH}} MHz. The ratio is {{CLK_RATIO}}×. The measured per-env-step cost ratio is {{COST_RATIO}}×. Those agree to {{CLK_GAP}}%.

Read that way the result stops being a surprise. The GH200 brings roughly twice the SMs and six times the memory, and this workload uses neither: it needs one narrow kernel to finish so the next can start. The estimate that predicted 1–2.5× faster was reading the specification sheet in the units of a dense-matmul workload.

Sizing rule for this stack

Rank candidate GPUs for mjwarp training by clock, not by FLOPs, memory or SM count, and confirm with a smoke job on a real scene before committing an allocation. A consumer part with a high boost clock will beat a datacenter part at the same generation on this workload, and the gap does not close at any batch size either machine can hold.

The clock figure for the GH200 is a specification, not a measurement — nobody has read clocks.sm on the compute node under load. That measurement is listed at the end.

This reframes what extra compute can buy. Adding a second GPU-equivalent to a machine running at {{DUTY}}% occupancy shortens nothing that was waiting on a GPU, because almost nothing was. The {{IDLE_DAYS}} idle days went to rendering filmstrips, reading trajectory health scorecards, bench sessions on the CB1, and deciding what the last result meant. Those are the serial resource.

Two caveats on the number. It counts only runs that logged five or more iterations, so crashed launches and short probes are missing, and it counts only RL training — CEM grasp synthesis, evaluation sweeps and rendering also use the card and are not in logs/*.log. The true occupancy is higher than {{DUTY}}%; it is not close to 100%.

The first cluster study removed the reason for the cluster

docs/experiments/B33_SEED_BAND.txt · {{SB_N}} draws, {{SB_GPUH}} GPU-hours, {{SB_SU}} SU

The allocation was sized around one quantity: the standard deviation between independent training draws of the same design. The program-wide prior was {{SB_SD_ASSUMED}}, taken from from-scratch reorient training, and at that value separating two designs by 0.2 in mean held-cos needs 48 seeds each. Twenty designs at that resolution is roughly 830 GPU-hours, which is what the whole allocation was for.

The seed-band study ran {{SB_N}} draws of b33's lineage on DeltaAI for {{SB_SU}} SU. The 15 core draws span 0.827 to 0.935 with a between-draw sd of {{SB_SD_CORE}} ({{SB_SD_ALL}} including the one drop-prone tail draw), against a within-draw rollout sd of {{SB_WITHIN}}. The training draw is less variable than the evaluation noise. The {{SB_SD_ASSUMED}} prior belongs to from-scratch training of a hard-exploration objective; warmstarting from the design's own Policy A removes the lottery.

{{TABLE_SEEDS}}

At the measured sd, the 20-design comparison that justified the allocation costs 16 GH200-hours, or under twelve hours on the workstation. The study answered its own question and priced its own successor out of needing a cluster. It also cost {{SB_SU}} SU and produced the second result the program needed: b33 sits at {{SB_REF}} with {{SB_RANK}} draws, so the sim-to-real hardware spec downstream of it rests on a representative policy rather than a lucky one.

One limit on how far that generalises. The seed band shared a single Policy A warmstart across all 16 draws. Whether sd {{SB_SD_CORE}} survives per-design from-scratch A training is untested, and a per-design search cannot reuse one hand's A on another's geometry — that substitution is what NaN'd the first attempt, 15 of 16 tasks diverging.

The timestep budget is a larger lever than the hardware

b33's 20M-timestep run, docs/rl/reorientation.md

Two budgets dominate the logged population: {{BUDGET_30M}} runs at 30M timesteps and {{BUDGET_20M}} at 20M. b33's own 20M run converged at iteration {{CONV_IT}} of {{CONV_BUDGET}}.

{{CHART_BUDGET}}

Cutting a converged run to its convergence point is worth about 10×. The best available hardware move, running on a GH200 instead, is {{RATIO_MED}}× in the wrong direction. Any plan that adds compute before trimming the budget is buying the smaller lever at the higher price. The trimming is not free of risk — a shorter budget on a from-scratch run is a different proposition than on a warmstarted finetune, and gotcha 13 applies, since the recipes carry reward shaping but not the timestep budget, so cutting it means editing every launcher that passes --total-timesteps explicitly.

Where the allocation still pays

Work that is genuinely wide and that nobody is waiting on: seed bands, hyperparameter or reward-shaping sweeps, ablations across many hands, and any question of the form “is this effect larger than the draw-to-draw noise”. The seed band is the model case. It was 9.4 hours of exclusive local GPU on a machine with one card, which is why it went unasked for two months, and it settled a question the whole sim-to-real spec depended on for 15 SU.

Where it does not

Anything with a person at the end of it. Per-run latency is worse, so a single run being watched belongs here. The A→B pipeline stays sequential per design because B warmstarts from A's checkpoint, so parallelism is across designs and seeds only, and chaining wants --dependency=afterok rather than lifting morph_pipeline_sweep.py onto a compute node. Rendering is worse than not cluster-shaped: EGL on the node image is untested and video encoding is billed GPU time, so filmstrips and eval videos come back to the workstation. And bulk training raises the risk the program is already most exposed to, since 48 seeds produce 48 reward curves and no pictures, while every conclusion in this repo has needed someone to look at a rollout.

What this does not settle

  1. Queue wait is unmeasured, and it decides the previous section. The {{IDEAL_SPEEDUP}}× figure assumes 16 GPUs start at once. Run sacct -j <jobid> --format=JobID,Submit,Start,Elapsed,Reserved,State after the next queue and take the median Submit→Start delta. If it exceeds about {{SEQ_LOCAL_H}} hours, breadth on the cluster is slower in wall clock than running the same work serially here.
  2. The GH200's clock under load has never been read. Add nvidia-smi --query-gpu=clocks.sm,clocks.max.sm,power.draw,utilization.gpu --format=csv -l 10 to the next deltaai_smoke.slurm. A reading near {{CLK_GH}} MHz confirms the latency explanation; a reading near {{CLK_LOC}} MHz falsifies it and sends the deficit back to memory subsystem or kernel dispatch.
  3. Billing granularity is still unresolved. The evidence is mixed: two short jobs moved the balance 92 → 91 → 91, while 13.9 hours of packed work drew {{SB_SU}} SU. Run one 8-hour packed block, wait a day, and re-read accounts. If charging is per reserved hour the packed queue in deltaai_pack_queue.slurm is correct and job arrays waste a tail per task; if it is per elapsed second the array is simpler and equally cheap.
  4. The convergence point is n = 1. {{CONV_IT}}-of-{{CONV_BUDGET}} comes from one run. Parse Metrics/.../target_axis_alignment out of the tensorboard logs of the {{BUDGET_20M}} runs at that budget and report the first iteration within 2% of each run's final value. A median convergence point above iteration 100 would make the trimming argument much weaker than it looks here.
  5. Whether the draw-to-draw sd holds without a shared warmstart. {{SB_SD_CORE}} was measured with all 16 draws warmstarted from the same a10 checkpoint. The test is a two-stage A-then-B queue on one hand, n = 4 from-scratch A per design, and it is the number that decides whether a per-design morphology search is affordable at all.
  6. The duty cycle is a floor, not a measurement of the card. It counts RL training logs only. Logging wall-clock GPU occupancy directly — nvidia-smi --query-gpu=utilization.gpu --format=csv -l 60 to a rotating file — would show how much of the {{IDLE_DAYS}} idle days is CEM, evaluation and rendering rather than genuinely idle.
