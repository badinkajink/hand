Ground-Supported Gaiting

A three-fingered hand turns a standing shaft {{TURNS}} full revolutions in forty grasp–twist–release cycles, open loop, with no policy and no sensing of the object. Take the floor away after the grasp is set and the identical command stream loses the shaft in the first cycle.

Where this sits

The program can stand the tool up. It has never let go of it.

Every reorientation result on this hand ends in the same posture: the shaft vertical, the grip fixed, and the three contacts sitting on the same material points they closed on. That is not incidental — it is the mechanism. The reorient was blocked by the grasp rather than the hand, and the fix was to raise the rotation axis above the contact plane so the fingers pay for the turn in retraction instead of extension. It reaches cos 0.996 open loop on this hand, with no controller beyond a straight line in joint space from the grasp anchor to one end pose.

Turning a screwdriver is the opposite problem. It is unbounded rotation about the tool's own axis, and three fingers with about twelve millimetres of tangential stroke cannot deliver it in one motion. It needs a gait: advance the contacts, let go, put them back, advance again. Letting go is the one thing every result in this program depends on not doing, which is why gaiting was catalogued and left alone.

The framing that makes it tractable is to stand the tool on the ground. A 100 mm shaft of 12.5 mm radius resting on its end face is statically stable until it tilts past atan(12.5/50) = 14.0°, so the fingers may leave it entirely and find it where they left it. The floor does not merely catch a dropped tool; it removes the failure mode that made the release unaffordable.

Hypothesis

With the shaft standing on the floor, a fixed set-point cycle of grasp–twist–release–return accumulates unbounded rotation about the shaft axis while the shaft stays upright and in place; and the ground contact is necessary, not merely convenient, so the same commands fail without it.

What is commanded

A tripod on a ring, and four phases

Three pads ride a ring around the standing shaft at azimuths of 180° for the thumb and ±60° for the pair — the same tripod the vertical-hold probe uses. The ring is commanded two millimetres inside the shaft surface, because these are position servos and the grip force is the commanded-minus-actual error; commanding a pad exactly onto the surface zeroes it and the hand lets go.

  1. 01twistall three azimuths advance by the stroke, carrying the shaft with them
  2. 02releasethe pads retract radially by 6 mm; the shaft is left standing on the floor
  3. 03returnthe azimuths run back to the start while retracted
  4. 04regraspthe pads close again onto the ring, on new material

The joint targets are solved once into a table indexed by ring radius and azimuth and then played back, so a rollout is pure integration and the command stream is a set-point list of the same shape the bench already runs. There is no policy here, no reward, and nothing reads the object.

Two geometric facts had to be fixed before any of it worked. The mounts are not symmetric about the palm origin — a ring centred there asks the thumb for 19.5 mm of horizontal reach and the pair for 36.9 mm — so the ring is offset 4 mm in +x, which brings every grip target inside 0.7 mm. And the approach has to come in radially at the ring height: ramping from the scene's open keyframe sweeps the pads 18 mm upward across the shaft and drags it 8 mm off the floor, which is the opposite of the condition under test.

The control, and the design split

The ground is necessary; so is being able to let go

Removing the floor at load time is a degenerate control — the shaft falls before the hand reaches it, so the cell measures nothing. Removing it once the grasp is established holds the grip, the press and the command stream fixed and varies only whether the ground is there during the release. Every hand that grips at all retains the shaft 6/6 with the floor and 0/6 without.

The table separates two different failures that a single success rate would have merged. rv00_wide, rv01_compact and rv02_narrowx cannot reach the gripping ring — 13 to 20 mm of residual, pad force under 0.05 N — so they never touch the shaft and the gait is not being measured on them at all. rv04_mid is the interesting one. It solves the gripping ring to 1.78 mm, holds the shaft for forty cycles at 34 N with a final tilt of 6°, and turns it 2.2° per cycle. It cannot release: 9.85 mm of residual on the retracted ring, and its pads are still on the shaft through the return stroke, which drags the shaft back as far as the twist advanced it.

That is a design criterion this program has not had before. The reorient's criterion was the thumb's moment arm and the extension budget; the gait's is whether the hand can withdraw its pads at the far end of the stroke. rv04_mid was the design the vertical-hold probe singled out as both reachable and commandable, and it is the one that gaits backwards.

Mechanism

The twist does the work, the regrasp gives some back

An early version of this measurement claimed the shaft gained 59° across the release and return with no hand contact at all. It was wrong twice over. The rotation was being integrated at the 50 Hz control rate over an angular velocity that changes at 500 Hz, and phase contact was sampled once at the end of each phase rather than throughout. The control that settled it is direct: spin the standing shaft at 10 rad/s with no hand present and it coasts 0.6° and stops in 12 ms. Nothing accumulates while the fingers are off the tool. What survives the correction is small and has a different cause: rv05_manual still shows +5.1° in the release phase at zero hand contact, which is the position servos still catching up to a twist set-point that has already reached its end value. The phase boundaries are command boundaries, not contact boundaries.

The other correction is geometric, and it changes what a good result looks like. The pad centres ride a ring of radius 21.05 mm about a shaft of radius 12.5 mm. A pad that sweeps dφ of azimuth translates tangentially by r_ring · dφ, and if it does not slip, the shaft surface must follow — so the shaft turns by (r_ring / r_obj) · dφ. That ratio is 1.684. A 30° stroke has a slip-free ceiling of 50.5° of shaft rotation, not 30°, and the first sweep's readings above 1.0 were this ratio rather than an artifact. Ratio-normalised, rv05_manual delivers 0.89 of the slip-free transmission.

Operating window

Some contact with the ground, and not too much

Press is how far the closed grip is driven down after the grasp is established: positive loads the shaft onto the floor, negative lifts it clear. It is a finger command, not a wrist move, so it is deliverable on a hand with no wrist.

The band is wide and the failure at the top is not an overload — ground normal force at +8 mm is 0.58 N against a 0.24 N tool. It is the asymmetric downward drag levering the shaft off its footprint. Transmission is flat to within 15% across the whole surviving band, so this is not a knob that needs tuning, which is the useful thing about it.

One caveat on the negative half: at −6 mm the grip lifts the shaft only about 7 mm, and the shaft falls that far during a 0.12 s release. Those cells are still ground-assisted. The floor-removal control is the only honest in-air comparison here.

Co-design

The fingertip radius is the gear ratio

Because the transmission is (r_obj + r_pad − squeeze) / r_obj, the pad radius is not a contact-mechanics detail here — it is the only term in the gear ratio the hand controls. A larger tip turns the tool further per degree of finger sweep.

The optimum sits at 13 mm — 52.6° per cycle against the shipped pad's 45.0°, a 17% gain for a part change and no control change. This is worth stating carefully because it runs against the fingertip verdict the reorientation work reached, where compact convex point contact won every comparison, every enlargement of the contact patch lost, and the radius optimum was 5–6 mm. Both results are about the same hand and the same tool. They disagree because the two tasks load the tip differently: the reorient is a roll, and a patch that resists rolling destroys it, whereas the gait is a tangential drive whose leverage grows with tip radius. A hand asked to do both has a real trade to make, and 13 mm is not obviously the right answer for the pair — it is the right answer for the half measured here.

Limits

What this does not show

  • The initial condition is granted, not chained. The shaft is placed upright on the floor under the hand rather than delivered there by the reorient. That is what the brief asked for, and the sim supports the palm pose it implies — six actuated palm degrees of freedom — but the seam between the carry's end state and this start state is untested.
  • No hardware. Nothing here has been exported through the plan mechanism or run past the trajectory-clearance and servo-limit gates, and no collision-safe gait plan exists. The per-joint travel this schedule asks for has not been checked against the family's measured ~1.34 rad clip ceiling.
  • A flat floor is not a screw. The load here is rim friction against a plane, 0.0072 N·m. A driven fastener resists with a torque that rises as it seats, and nothing in this study says where the gait stalls against a real one.
  • Contact stiffness is the sim's. The reorient on this hand was measured to be one-sidedly fragile to pad friction, shaft diameter and contact stiffness. Those axes have not been swept here, and the gait's dependence on them is unknown rather than absent.
  • Ten hands, one object. Six of the ten never grip the vertical ring, so the design conclusion rests on four. Spawn jitter is 0.5 mm; no pose error, mass or friction randomisation was applied.

Next

The three things worth doing

Chain it. Run the raised-pivot carry to its vertical end state, then set the palm down onto the floor and hand the gait the pose it actually produces. The carry is known to be sensitive at the 1e-6 level and its end state is a distribution, not a point, so this is a real test rather than plumbing.

Gate it for the bench. The clearance gate, the servo-limit check and the plan export already exist; a gait plan is a longer set-point list than a carry plan and nothing about it is new except its length. Forty cycles at 50 Hz is about ninety seconds of bus time, well inside what the station sustains, but sustained cyclic loading is exactly the regime the servos' overload protection exists for.

Make the release a design variable. It is the criterion that separates the hands here, it is cheap to evaluate — one IK table, no rollout — and no existing screen measures it.
