Defect · what the shipped MJCF did silently

Three things had to be fixed before any number meant anything

Your geometry was right. What was missing was the scaffolding that makes it measurable, and each gap fails quietly rather than loudly.

The fingertip bodies had no geoms. The RL contact sensor matches by body on thumb_tip, index_tip, middle_tip. A body with no geom can never appear in a contact, so every fingertip-contact reward, the tip-loss termination and the grip-force scorecard would have read exactly zero for a whole training run — indistinguishable from a policy that never touched the object. The distal capsule's terminal cap now lives on the tip body as a sphere at the same place, so the envelope is unchanged and the pad is its own body.

223 N of interpenetration at rest. The yaw capsule's cap sits inside the palm plate, and the 12.70 mm link overhang means the yaw and distal links genuinely overlap. MuJoCo only auto-filters direct parent/child pairs, so both showed up as permanent contact forces — 223 N against the palm and 38 N per finger — before a policy had done anything. 21 <contact><exclude> entries; the hand now rests at 0.000 N.

The palm was at the old hand's height. 0.134 m was fitted to a 117 mm finger. This one reaches 78.66 mm, so the fingertips stopped 30 mm above a shaft lying on the table and no grasp existed at all. Palm pose is the arm's pose, not a hardware parameter, so it is now solved per design.

Everything the CAD does fix — link lengths, the 12.70 mm overlap, ROM, mounts, gantry travel — is taken from your model verbatim, and pinned by 13 tests so an edit cannot quietly move the model away from the hardware.

Root cause · the slip you spotted

The grip did not slide. It opened.

You saw the screwdriver creeping out during Policy A. The trainer agreed: tip_lost was firing 1.4–2.3 times per batch while object_height sat at 0.106 against a 0.10 target — the lift succeeded and then the grip decayed. That pattern is contact, not control, so it should reproduce with no policy at all. It does, and what it shows is not slipping.

The cause is where the pads sit on the shaft. All three landed +10.1° above the shaft's axis, on the upper hemisphere. There the contact normals tilt up and outward, so the wedge drives the shaft down out of the pinch and only friction opposes it — against a normal force that shrinks as the shaft creeps down and the pads ride toward the top of the cylinder, where the surface curves away. Positive feedback: stable through the entire lift, unwound in 0.6 s of standing still.

That --elevation 10 was my own default, chosen because a 21 mm-wide pad cannot get under a 25 mm shaft on a table without going through the floor. At elevation 0 the pad's underside clears the floor by 2.0 mm, which is enough.

Making the pads compliant does not help and makes it slightly worse — a soft pad on the upper hemisphere sinks in and slides off more easily. Torsional and rolling friction change nothing because nothing is rotating. Quadrupling sliding friction changes nothing because the shaft is not sliding against the pads; they have released it. Your instinct to lower the palm was the right one, and it is the same fix as the elevation reached from the other end: 5 mm down moves the pads from 4.25 mm above the axis to about a millimetre below it.

Refitting at elevation 0 holds +60 mm where +10 holds nothing (−0.10 mm at every candidate palm height). Below the equator also works but costs grip depth — 52.0 mm at −8° against a 50 mm shaft half-length, so 2 mm of clearance headroom, versus 55.5 mm at 0°. Elevation 0 is now the default.

Your hand-authored grasp puts the pads at +1.9 / −3.2 / +7.5° — on the equator, without seeing any of this. Eyeballing the grasp put the contacts where the wedge works; the reachability-driven fitter did not.

Design set · six hands

Your workspace has three knobs but two dimensions

Because the fitter re-centres palm X over the shaft, thumb forward and pair back are the same hand: both land at 70 mm separation, 68.0 mm of grip depth, and identical joint angles. Only two things actually vary, so the sampled set is a 2×2 factorial in those plus a centre — and your hand-authored design sits outside it, asymmetric in a way the knobs cannot express.

The pinch has a minimum width, and the object sets it. Pads on the shaft's equator sit on a ring 48.1 mm across for a 25 mm shaft and a 21 mm pad. A hand whose thumb and pair are 40 mm apart would have to place each pad outboard of its own mount, and flexion only moves a fingertip inboard. Both X = 40 designs fail, at every straddle and every palm height. That bounds the X dimension of your gantry travel from below by the object rather than by the hand — a smaller shaft opens it back up.

One thing worth knowing before reading any per-design number: because the fitter places fingertips at world targets, every design that can reach them grasps the shaft at the same three points with the same pad geometry. The designs differ in the joint configuration that gets there — effective stiffness at the contact, and what the hand can do next — not in where they touch. Your rv05_manual is the exception: its pose is yours, stored verbatim, a hook with negative MCP and strong PIP flexion rather than the fitter's pinch.

Prediction · recorded before the policies exist

Wide straddle should be reorient-hostile

The pinch axis is X and the shaft lies along Y, so standing the shaft up means rotating it about the pinch axis. The thumb sits at y=0 and acts as the pivot; index and middle straddle it and have to drive the rotation differentially, one tip up and one tip down. A 90° turn moves each pair contact by its own Y offset in Z:

Against 78.66 mm of total flexing reach, while keeping the grip.

Verdict: cannot resolve, with one piece of support from an unexpected direction

The trained policies cannot test it. All three peak cosines are under 0.07, so there is no reorientation for a wide straddle to be hostile to, and the ordering that does exist (0.069 at ±25 mm, 0.019 at ±55, and the only positive held-cos on the narrow one) sits far inside this program's from-scratch draw spread of 0.3–0.5. Read that as noise.

The kinematic probe is more interesting. The widest straddle is the one that cannot reach the vertical hold at all, and the residual shrinks monotonically as the straddle narrows — 15.8 mm at ±55, 8.8 at ±25, 2.5 at ±40. Monotonically in the straddle, that is, except that ±40 beats ±25, so the mechanism is not purely the Z-travel argument the prediction was built on: rv04_mid also has the deepest grip (65 mm against 52–53), which buys the palm clearance the standing shaft needs. Straddle and grip depth are confounded across this set.

So: the prediction survives, is not confirmed, and the experiment that would settle it is a straddle sweep at fixed grip depth — which the fitter can now do, because palm height is an explicit solved variable rather than an inherited one.

Lift is 0.10 m, so the shaft's lower end sits at z = 0.0625 when vertical. This is a true in-hand reorient with no floor to brace against — unlike m05 and perp, where the August 26 primitive study found 69% and 46% of the alignment gain happened while the shaft still touched the table.

The grasp fix survives contact with a policy

All three hold the shaft at 0.105–0.116 m through the whole post-handoff phase, with all three fingers in contact 100% of the time and under 3 mm of net drift. I watched each filmstrip: the hand lifts clear of the table and the shaft stays in the grip for the full 4.8 s. The same hand, before the elevation fix, leaked it in 0.6 s. That closes the defect you spotted.

And none of them rotates it, at all

Peak alignment cosine 0.015 to 0.069 — under four degrees on the best of them. The shaft comes up horizontal and stays horizontal in every filmstrip. What Policy B did instead was squeeze: on rv00_wide the tip force went from 9.7 N after A to 17.4 N after B while the cosine stayed at zero, with slide ratio 23.9 → 35.2. That is a policy banking the contact and stability terms because the term it was actually paid for never fired.

Grip force tracks straddle width almost exactly — 5.6 N at ±25 mm, 13.9 at ±40, 17.4 at ±55. The wide-straddle hands clamp three times harder to hold the same object. That is a real morphology effect and it is the only one this night's data actually resolves.

Why the reorient reward never fired

A reward reading flat zero has two very different causes, and the reward table cannot tell them apart: the behaviour is unattractive, or it cannot be commanded. A residual policy drives the fingers to closed_ctrl + 0.5 × a, so any pose more than 0.5 rad from the CEM grip on any single joint is outside the action space entirely. So I asked, directly: can each hand ring a vertical shaft at the height the handoff holds it, and is that pose inside the budget?

Only rv04_mid is both reachable and commandable, and it is the one to push on. This is the failure mode that cost this program three runs before, and it means finger_residual_scale is the first knob to move — not the reward weights, and not the morphology.

Reference

Geometry, as modelled

Phalange half-width 10.55 mm. Yaw and middle links 33.45 mm axis-to-surface, distal 37.16 mm axis-to-pad. Consecutive links overlap 12.70 mm, so joint spacing is 20.75 mm and total flexing reach is 78.66 mm. Yaw ±85°, MCP −15°…+92°, PIP −18°…+92°. Mounts at thumb (−50, 0), index (+50, +55), middle (+50, −55) mm; gantry travel 60×110 for the thumb and 60×60 for the pair. No proximal-length DoF — the links are CAD parts, so the design space is the six XY dimensions.

What to run next

1. Raise the residual budget and re-run rv04_mid. It is the one design that can both reach and command the vertical hold, and it still needs 0.465 of its 0.5 rad. That is no headroom to explore in. Try --finger-residual-scale 0.8 on B, or re-centre closed_ctrl between the grasp and the vertical hold with --closed-ctrl-from-keyframe. Check with probe_real_v1_vertical_hold.py before spending the GPU hours, not after.

2. Separate straddle from grip depth. The two are confounded across this design set, and grip depth is now a solved variable rather than an inherited one, so a straddle sweep at fixed depth is cheap to build and would actually settle the prediction.

3. Seeds before designs. One from-scratch draw per design against a known spread of 0.3–0.5 cannot rank anything. Whatever the residual-budget change does, it needs two or three draws on the same design before it means anything.

The compliance question is still open, but it is a separate one: TPU pads were ruled out as a fix for this failure, not as a model of the hardware. The honest test is a stiffness sweep on a grasp that already holds — which now exists.
