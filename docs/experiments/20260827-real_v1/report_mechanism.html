Four hands, one answer. The grasp was the reason none of them could turn.

Overnight, four real_v1 designs carried the screwdriver through the A→B handoff without a wobble and rotated it by under four degrees. Their mounts differ by 60 mm. A result that survives that much change of hand is not a morphology result, and at four draws it is not the seed lottery either — so it is upstream of both. It was, and the pose fitter says why in its own docstring.

Last night I read this as an action-space problem: the vertical hold sits outside the ±0.5 rad residual budget on two of three designs. That was true and it was secondary. rv04_mid was inside its budget, at 0.465 of 0.5, and turned the shaft 0.9°.

Mechanism

A turn is paid for in reach, and there was none left

Rotate the shaft by θ about the pinch axis with the pads staying on the same material points. The pair contact on the descending side moves down by straddle · sin θ, and the only way its finger follows is to extend. So the achievable turn is bounded by how much reach that finger has left, divided by the straddle.

Both quantities are measurable with no policy and no physics. The chain from mount to pad is 68.11 mm straight (20.75 yaw link + 20.75 + 26.61), and each design's grasp sits somewhere inside it:

Provenance

Both halves of that bound were my choices

The palm sits as deep as it can reach. fit_real_v1_pose searches for the deepest palm height that still reaches, because grip depth is what clears the shaft's upper half once it stands up. On this hand that lands every design at 58–67 mm of a 68.11 mm chain — the top of the band, where a fingertip has no radial room left.

The straddle is scored on resisting exactly this rotation. From the fitter's own comment: “whether that tripod resists the shaft levering about the pinch axis depends on the straddle…” Levering about the pinch axis is the reorientation. Candidates were being ranked, per design, by how well they prevent the task.

The obvious repair — raise the palm, un-extend the fingers — does not exist on this hand. Below about 60 mm of grip depth the MCP pins against its −15° hyperextension limit and no pose is reachable at all:

So the extension budget is close to a hardware fact for a 25 mm shaft grasped from above with the pads on the equator. It is not something a different mount layout rescues, which is why all four designs read the same.

The fix

Rotate about an axis above the contacts, and the bill moves to the other side

Put the rotation axis h above the contact plane instead of through it. Both pair contacts then gain h(1 − cos θ). Choose h and the descending contact needs no extension at all — the cost lands on the ascending finger, as retraction, of which there is 38–47 mm. Physically it is the shaft sliding down in the grip as it stands up.

Driven open-loop, no policy: hold the palm at the full 0.10 m handoff height, rotate the contacts as a rigid body about the raised axis, IK each fingertip to its own new point, and apply the result as a residual on the grasp anchor.

That last clause is the part worth keeping. REORIENT_PRIMITIVE.txt measured 46–69% of both reference policies' alignment gain as work done while the shaft was still touching the floor. This is the first turn in the program with no floor in it.

Three things that gave a convincing wrong answer first

Direction is not free. Turning the other way stands the shaft up at cos −0.82, and target_axis_alignment pays for cos → +1, not |cos|. The first training smoke of this schedule read target_axis_progress −0.93 for that reason alone, and I nearly read it as the anchor not working.

A straight line in joint space reproduces the IK'd carry — 0.82 against 0.76 on rv03_narrowy. So the whole schedule collapses to one extra set-point, which is exactly LerpFingerActionCfg.hold_target_ctrl: already in the repo, written for the opposed hand, never used here.

The anchor move has to be bounded. Clipped at ±0.5 rad it holds. Unclipped at ±1.5 it drops the shaft in every single cell. The bound is load-bearing, not an artifact of the residual budget it was borrowed from.

And the first probe I wrote measured the wrong thing. An external-torque sweep said the grip “breaks away” at 0.010 Nm — but the rows with object z 0.048 and two contacts at 0.2 N are a shaft that fell out and landed standing on the table. Peak cosine without height and contact count is not a result; that trap is written down in REORIENT_PRIMITIVE.txt and I walked into it anyway.

The perp schedule really does transfer — 0.848 held on three fingers, on a hand it has never seen, arrived at by a completely different route than the geometric carry and picking out the same design. b33's rolling schedule does not: its 0.995 comes with final z 0.0124, which is the shaft standing on the table.

But the transferred schedule does not beat one computed from the object's own rigid-body kinematics, and it only works where the workspace already allowed it. That is consistent with what the primitive study concluded — what transfers is the description, not the generator, because a third to a half of each reference behaviour is authored by the floor and by gravity on an off-centre grip rather than by the fingertips. Here there is no floor and the grip is centred, so there is nothing to import: the schedule has to be generated, and rotating the contacts as a rigid body about a chosen axis generates all of it.

A design metric, for once

One forward-kinematics call, and it agrees with the carry nine times out of nine

This program has looked for a cheap per-design reorientability score before and the honest answer has been no. reorient_primitive.py feasibility asks whether a hand can put its fingertips on a recorded schedule and every design lands within 0.2–2.2 mm on a 12.5 mm shaft — reachability is saturated, so it cannot rank anything. The UHAS authority metric ranked the grip and correlated −0.03 with goal approach.

What was missing is that a reorient is not a reachability question, it is a budget question, and the budget has a closed form. Sweeping the two dimensions the workspace actually has, at a fixed straddle, with no CEM and no policy — about a minute per design on CPU:

The budget is set almost entirely by thumb-to-pair X separation, and Y separation barely moves it: 7.2 / 7.2 / 7.1 mm across the whole Y range. A wide hand has to reach inward to the shaft, so its fingers slant and keep reach in hand; a compact hand hangs them straight down at full extension and has nothing left to spend. That is the opposite of the intuition that a compact layout is the manipulable one, and it is a hardware statement: keep the X gantries wide.

Two honesties about this table. The carries here use the fitter's geometric grip rather than a CEM one and only three pivot heights, so they are a floor — the X 100 / Y 50 cell reads 0.575 here and 0.909 when tuned with its CEM grip. And the score is a ceiling, so it can only ever say a design cannot; that a design clears it does not mean a policy will find the behaviour.

Training

The reorient becomes a residual problem

The one gap between the open-loop carry and the trainer was that hold_target_ctrl latches on alignment: the anchor only moves once the policy has rotated the shaft. When the grasp is a rotational lock that never opens, which is why a mechanism already sitting in the codebase had never fired here. New hold_switch_from_sim_step opens it on schedule instead, so Policy B rides a moving set-point the way Policy A rides the scripted lift.

Recipe b_liveA plus --hold-ctrl-from-keyframe hold_ik --hold-switch-from-sim-step 600 --hold-switch-steps 550 --hold-switch-min-z 0.08 --object-orientation-drift-weight 0.0. The last of those was paying the policy −3 per radian to keep the shaft at its spawn orientation. Tip-loss termination is relaxed from 3 to 15 control steps, because the carry runs on two contacts for most of the turn exactly as r4 does on perp; the drop termination and the collapse watchdog are untouched.

Runs in flight; this section is filled in when they land.

What I would do next

1. Judge it against --zero-b, not against last night. With the anchor block in place, a zero-action B is the scripted carry riding Policy A's real delivery. The claim worth making is the gap between the trained policy and that control, and eval_real_v1_anchor.py runs both.

2. Make the anchor a schedule, not an endpoint. A single second set-point already reproduces the carry, but the fitted axis height differs per design (5.8–15.5 mm) and the trace shows the shaft rocking through the middle of the turn. A phase-indexed anchor — the object-centric representation used as the anchor rather than as a shaping reward — is the obvious next version.

3. Re-fit the grasp for the turn, not against it. fit_real_v1_pose should stop scoring straddle candidates purely on hold, and --axial-offset now exists precisely so that a shallower, less-extended grasp can buy back its palm clearance by gripping off-centre. The extension budget is a design metric that costs seconds and has, on four designs, ordered them correctly.

4. Two or three draws before ranking anything. Unchanged, and still the constraint on every morphology claim in this program.

Nothing here required changing the finger geometry, and nothing here contradicts last night: the hold really is solved, and the numbers that looked like a morphology verdict really were one — just about the grasp we imposed on the hand, rather than the hand.
