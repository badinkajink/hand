MorphoHand · in-hand reorientation

Two finger arrangements, and the trade between turning and holding

Rotating a screwdriver to vertical and keeping hold of it pull in opposite directions, and the two hand arrangements tried sit on opposite sides of that trade. The opposed pair reaches a near-perfect vertical in every rollout and then loses the object every time, because the rotation and the loss are the same slide through a two-point pinch. The inline pair opposing the thumb reorients less far, to about cos 0.9, and holds it in a real three-finger grasp.

Neither is finished. This page states what each arrangement actually buys, why the opposed pair's failure is structural rather than a tuning problem, and what would have to change to get both properties at once.

Part one

What each arrangement buys

The headline numbers point in opposite directions, and reading either one alone gives the wrong impression of where the program stands.

The opposed pair's alignment number is the best in the program and it is also the least useful, because the mechanism that produces it is the mechanism that ends it. The shaft turns by sliding through the pinch, so a rotation that completes is a rotation that has spent the pad. Every rollout ends with the screwdriver on the floor. The thumb registers 0 N throughout and is not merely unused — it cannot reach the shaft at any pinch offset that still permits the swing.

The opposed pair is not a better hand that needs more tuning. It converts the reorientation into a one-shot expenditure of pad length, and buys its alignment number with the grasp.

The inline arrangement gives up the last 10° of rotation and keeps a working three-finger grip. For anything that has to hold the tool afterwards, that is the better trade.

On the horizons, so the comparison is fair

Both eventually lose the object, but not comparably. The opposed pair drops at step 389 ± 25 against a 600-step training horizon — it fails 65% of the way into the episode it was trained on. The inline pair holds through its own 250-step horizon and past it to 300; pushed to 600, which is 2.4× what it was trained for, it also slides out. Neither holds indefinitely, and only one fails inside its own training distribution.

The rest of this page is about the opposed pair, because it is the arrangement whose failure is now understood well enough to say what would fix it. The inline arrangement's own open problems — de-centering, over-clamping, and a reorientation that stalls near cos 0.9 — are unchanged from the earlier work and are summarised in Part 3.

Part two

How the opposed pair works, and where it ends

The mechanism, the policy that exploits it, and the measurements that say it should survive contact with hardware.

2.1The mechanism

Two fingertips pressed on opposite sides of a cylinder sit on its axis of rotation. They can carry the shaft but exert almost no torque about that axis, so the pinch behaves as a low-friction pin joint. On a conventional hand that is a defect. Here it is the whole design.

The pair grips the shaft about 35 mm from its centre of mass. Gravity then supplies a moment about the pinch, the long side swings down, and the shaft ends hanging vertically. The object spawns along world X, so its body axis starts horizontal and "long end down" is exactly the target orientation. Nothing about the rotation itself has to be learned.

The window is narrow, and it is the one variable that governs the outcome. Measured open-loop by varying clamp depth alone, with the pinch symmetric: below about 3 N the shaft drops; between 4 and 9 N it swings to vertical; above about 10 N the friction torque about the pinch axis exceeds gravity's moment, the pin joint becomes a rigid clamp, and the rotation is choked off.

The last column matters for a practical reason. A grasp synthesised by CEM lands at roughly 21 N per finger, because force closure rewards clamping hard and breaking symmetry to wedge the object. That objective is directly opposed to this mechanism. Seeding the policy from an authored symmetric keyframe at 8 N instead of the CEM grasp moves the starting point from outside the window to inside it, and is worth more than any reward weight.

2.2One policy, not two

The program's standing architecture splits lift and reorientation into two policies, A and B, with a handoff. On this topology that split is what breaks the task, and the evidence is a control rather than an argument: two independently trained B policies, one warm-started and one from scratch, both eject the shaft at the switch step, while the same A driving the entire rollout with no switch holds it for the full horizon with no lateral drift.

The reason is the narrow window above. The grip spans hold-without-rotation to dropped over about 9 mm of pad set-point, and it is held by a two-point pinch with no pitch resistance, so any discontinuity in finger targets at the switch lands outside the window. Lift and reorientation are the same motion here, so they are trained as one policy and the seam simply does not exist.

What the reward had to get right

Four things, none of which is a weight on the reorientation term:

  • Stop paying to hold the object still. Two base terms rewarded matching the spawn orientation and penalised drifting from it, together outweighing the reorientation reward by roughly fifteen to one. Both are zeroed.
  • Start the signed reward before the decision. The pole the shaft turns towards is committed between steps 20 and 50, and the only signed term was gated to step 65. It was a spectator to the one decision that mattered.
  • Keep a gradient on the wrong half of the sphere. The alignment term exp(-α(1-cos)²) at α=4 is around 1e-7 for any negative cos, so a full-magnitude rotation to the wrong pole scored the same as never moving. α=2 keeps the two distinguishable.
  • Make the grip a schedule the policy learns. The swing needs a loose pinch; the shaft then reaches vertical by sliding out of one, so the grip must firm up afterwards. One always-on weight cannot ask for both.

That last gate is worth isolating, because getting it wrong twice is what produced the method. It was first written as a step count, at 150 of a manoeuvre that finished at 484, and then at 450, by which point episodes had shortened to 165 and the gate never fired at all. With no reachable way to hold on, the only remaining way to stop losing the shaft was to stop rotating, and PPO took it.

Phase gates belong on task progress, not on the clock. A step count has to be guessed from the previous run's trajectory, and it is invalidated by the very learning it exists to shape.

The working version fires the grip term when alignment passes cos 0.7, however long that took. It cannot drift, because it is defined by the thing it is waiting for.

2.3What it does

Deterministic policy, 64 parallel rollouts on the current scene. The shaft is picked off the floor, rotated to vertical, and held there by the fingers rather than resting on anything.

The distribution is what the claim rests on. Single rollouts of this stack are not reproducible — three deterministic rollouts of one checkpoint, with sampling off and spawn jitter at zero, ended three different ways, because parallel contact solves do not reduce in a fixed order on GPU. 2.4Why it should transfer

Two properties were measured specifically because they are what usually breaks an in-hand manipulation policy on hardware.

It does not depend on contact compliance

Nothing degrades; the stiff end is marginally better. This is the opposite of the same program's baseline hand, whose reorientation collapses under identical hardening. The mechanism accounts for the difference. The baseline reorients by rolling the cylinder across deforming pads, which spends compliance directly; the opposed pair pivots about two contacts and does not. A contact-stiffness curriculum is a prerequisite for the baseline hand and is not one here.

It fits inside the prototype's actuators

The MJCF joint ranges are wider than the hardware in every degree of freedom, which says nothing on its own, since the policy may never approach them. Asking the rollout instead: every joint travels less than the prototype allows, and no joint's 99th-percentile rate exceeds 70 °/s. The opposed pair works in a notably small envelope — index MCP 54–62°, PIP 30–41°, yaw inside ±13°.

One joint flags: thumb_pip operates over −98.5° to −85.6° against a hardware range of 0–100°. That is 13° of travel inside a 100° servo, sitting at a different zero because the thumb is mounted mirrored. It needs an agreed offset, not a larger actuator.

The transfer risk is concentrated in mounting, not in control or contact physics. The 90° opposition is a fixed mount rotation, not the yaw joint, and the yaw servo spans ±45° — so the pair cannot be brought into opposition by any command. The fingers have to be physically remounted rotated 90° on their carriages.

A second question is open and cheap to answer: the mounts sit at |y| = 48 mm against a specified base-Y travel of ±40 mm, and the thumb at x = −65 against ±60. Whether that is a real conflict depends on where each gantry's zero sits relative to the palm origin, which cannot be checked from the simulation side.

2.5What is not solved

Every rollout eventually loses the shaft, at step 389 ± 25. The cause is a slow slide of the object down through the pinch. Measured in the palm frame, the shaft travels 44.8 ± 0.8 mm through the grip over the hold; the spread of under a millimetre across 64 rollouts identifies it as a mechanism rather than noise. The world frame understates it as roughly 12 mm, because the palm is moving as well.

The obvious response is to charge for the slide, and it was implemented and swept: a per-step, one-sided, contact-gated penalty on downward motion of the object in the palm frame. It behaves exactly as specified. At the high weight the object-height trace goes flat for 400 steps — the slide is gone. It still fails, and the way it fails is the useful part.

At −1000 the object is retained 32% longer and the rotation becomes far more consistent (spread in time-to-alignment falls from 97 steps to 20), but the rotation takes 200 steps longer, so the window in which the shaft is both vertical and held is shorter than r4's. At −3000 the shaft is held rigidly at cos 0.45 and never finishes turning.

The reason both outcomes are the same outcome is in the calibration. The slide splits into 23.8 mm before alignment reaches cos 0.7, in only about 44 steps, and 20.4 mm after, over about 349 steps. More than half of the travel is the rotation.

Rotation and retention are not two objectives to be balanced. They are the same degree of freedom: the shaft turns because it travels through the pinch.

Any penalty on that travel buys hold time by spending rotation, monotonically, at roughly a neutral exchange rate. The pad length is a budget, and the reorientation spends it.

This retires reward tuning as a route to a longer hold. Extending it needs a mechanism that returns pad rather than conserving it — a regrasp or finger gait that re-seats the shaft upward between rotation increments, or a geometry whose budget is larger. Both sit outside the reward function.

For a minimum hardware demonstration r4 remains the policy, with about five seconds of held vertical. The −1000 variant is the better choice only if a demonstration needs the object retained longer more than it needs it upright sooner.

Part three · appendix

Where the problem, the method and the alternatives came from

Background for the choices above: what the earlier architecture was, how each element of the method was forced by a specific failure, and how the alternatives measure against it.

AThe problem this replaced

The task throughout has been the same: pick up a screwdriver lying flat and bring it to vertical using the fingers alone, with no wrist and no external support. The earlier work used a conventional three-finger layout on a co-designed palm, split into a lifting policy and a reorienting policy. It produced a working pipeline and two problems that resisted a long sequence of fixes.

The handoff seam. A reorienting policy trained against a teleported initial state is out of distribution at the moment the lifting policy actually hands over, and drops the object. This was eventually closed for the baseline hand by driving the real lifting policy live during the reorienter's training, with the pre-handoff steps masked out of the update. It took a large number of attempts, and it is the single most expensive item in the program's history.

Compliance dependence. The baseline reorientation works by rolling the cylinder between fingertips. Hardening contact towards something more physically realistic causes it to fail while the grasp and lift survive a retrain: the alignment reward falls from 48 to 13, and the policy holds the object without turning it. Evaluating fixed policies across a stiffness range showed no policy was robust and the response was not even monotonic. Domain randomisation over compliance was the recommended next step, and was not resolved.

The opposed-finger topology was introduced as an exploratory change and removes both problems rather than solving them. There is no seam because there are not two policies, and there is no compliance dependence because the reorientation is a pivot rather than a roll. What it removes them at the cost of is the grasp, which is the subject of Part 1.

Three open problems, all long-standing. The reorientation stalls near cos 0.9 rather than completing. The object slides around under the fingers — eight centimetres of travel to reach a net displacement of one — which is the de-centering the co-refinement work reduced but did not remove. And the grip is harder than it needs to be, though the earlier chase after grip force on this hand turned out to be measuring the wrong thing and should not be resumed without a specific reason.

Set against the opposed pair, none of these is fatal, and the thing it does have is the thing the opposed pair cannot get: three fingers carrying load on an object it keeps hold of.

BHow the method was arrived at

Each element of Part 1 was forced by a specific measured failure. In order:

  1. A onlyThe lifting recipe's orientation-slip termination fires on the gravity pitch, which is the task. The reliable way to avoid rotating is not to lift at all, and PPO found exactly that; a watchdog on object height caught it at iteration 44. Aggregate reward never showed it. Relaxing the termination was necessary and nowhere near sufficient.
  2. A → BTwo independently trained reorienters, one warm-started and one from scratch, both eject the shaft at the switch. A control with the lifting policy driving the whole rollout passes every check. The seam is the cause, which makes the single-stage design a measured result rather than a preference.
  3. SingleFirst single-stage attempt still clamps, peak cos 0.015. The per-term table shows two base rewards paying to hold the spawn orientation, outweighing the reorientation term about fifteen to one. Both zeroed.
  4. Wrong poleNext attempt reads as "still will not rotate", alignment flat near 0.03. That reading was wrong. It rotates at full magnitude to the opposite pole — held cos −0.449, 28 of 32 rollouts negative. The alignment term was flat across the entire negative half of the sphere, so a wrong-way rotation and no rotation scored the same. Found by rendering the policy, not by reading the table.
  5. ReleaseThe scripted probe's headline result — vertical and airborne — turns out to be a short-horizon artifact. Run 800 steps longer, grip force and alignment are anti-correlated to zero: the shaft reaches vertical by sliding out of the pinch and ends standing on the floor, reporting a perfect 1.000. Two verdict functions were corrected to ask the physics instead.
  6. r2First trained policy that reorients without releasing: alignment climbs monotonically to +0.947 with grip maintained at 10–27 N. It walks the shaft between the pads rather than letting it go, and takes about four times as long as the free swing. That cost is the price of holding on.
  7. r3Longer horizon with the grip gate moved to step 450. Peaks at iteration 50 and degrades monotonically to a divergence at 188 as episodes shorten below the gate. Both step-gate attempts failed, in opposite directions, which is the argument for gating on task progress.
  8. r4Alignment-gated grip. Improves monotonically end to end and is the policy in Part 1.

Two infrastructure faults in the same period are worth recording because both silently produce plausible numbers. Exploration noise at the framework default diverges this scene within a few steps, and one environment in 512 going non-finite aborts an entire run. Separately, requiring all three fingertips in contact is unsatisfiable on this hand — the thumb cannot reach the shaft at any pinch offset that still permits the swing — so every episode terminated three steps after the lift, with two reward rows sitting in the table looking active while being structurally incapable of taking a non-zero value. Relaxing the requirement to two contacts moved mean episode length from 62 to 149.

One further comparison is worth stating because it inverts a standing conclusion. On the baseline hand, grip force was measured to be decoupled from both hold quality and jitter, and the guidance has been to stop treating it as a lever. That does not carry over. On the opposed pair force is the dominant variable, because it sets whether the pinch behaves as a pin joint or a clamp. A conclusion of this kind is topology-specific and does not transfer with the codebase.

Morphology search: no valid numbers yet

An open-loop ranking over 50 variants of the mount layout put the shipped design second, and a training queue over the top five was launched to confirm it. That queue is void. The launcher never passed the lift-height flag, so every design trained at 0.05 m against the reference policies' 0.14 m. On a 100 mm shaft that puts the hanging end in the floor and false-triggers the floor-proximity termination.

Exactly one design ran to completion before the queue was interrupted, and evaluating it gives an alignment rate of 0.0% with every rollout ending flat on the floor. There are therefore no valid morphology numbers for this topology, and the open-loop ranking has never been confirmed by training. The queue has been corrected and relaunched.

Why single rollouts are not used as evidence anywhere above

Three deterministic rollouts of one checkpoint, with stochastic sampling disabled and spawn jitter set to zero, ended three different ways: held at step 400, lost at 430, and lost before 400. The actions are deterministic, so the spread comes from the simulator — parallel contact solves do not reduce in a fixed order on GPU.

Every headline figure here is over 64 rollouts stepped together, which costs about the same wall-clock as one. Renders are included to be looked at, not measured.

Metric traps specific to this task

A perfect alignment score is compatible with total failure. A 100 mm shaft standing upright on the floor sits with its centre at 0.050 m and reports cos 1.000. A drop termination written as z < 0.05 never fires on it, so it is a reachable reward hack, and specifically a hazard when strengthening the reorientation reward. Holding vertical sits at about 0.113 m, so the threshold was raised to 0.09.

A success rate at a fixed step ranks slower policies higher. Truncating before a slow policy finishes failing flatters it. Compare on the length of the vertical-and-held run instead, and evaluate past the point where both policies have resolved — the tables above use 900 steps for that reason.

Minimum height over a whole rollout is dominated by the pre-lift state. The object starts on the floor, so the minimum reads about 0.012 m on a policy that never drops anything. Use the post-lift minimum.

DReproduction

The policy is results/rl/20260731-1300-perp_single_r4, checkpoint 338, trained single-stage on the perpendicular scene at a lift height of 0.14 m. Evaluation, renders and the joint-envelope check are driven by scripts/policy_eval_suite.py, scripts/rl_render_reorient.py and scripts/probe_joint_envelope.py.

Launcher configurations are checked against the reference run before training rather than after. Four separate experiments in this program have been lost to a launcher omitting a flag and the trainer silently supplying a default, so the config the trainer writes on startup is now diffed against a known-good run, and a divergent run is stopped while it is still free. Differences that are intended must be declared explicitly, which puts them in the launcher where they are visible in review.
