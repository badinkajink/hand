Clip, Cap and Stall

Three things bound the turn this hand can perform, and until today we had measured none of them. One is a number inherited from a reinforcement-learning policy that has nothing to do with open-loop control. One is a servo range. The third is the hand refusing to go where it is told.

The inherited number

Every morphology screen this program has run was measured at one residual clip, and nobody chose it

The open-loop plan is a list of joint angles against time. Each joint's motion away from its grasp anchor is capped at a per-joint limit called the residual clip — and that limit has been 0.5 rad, hardcoded, in the 108-hand search, the 128-hand pilot and the 4,096-hand retention screen alike.

0.5 rad is Policy B's residual action budget: a cap on how far a learned residual may push a joint away from a reference. It is a reinforcement-learning safety rail. It has no business constraining a scripted trajectory, and it got here by inheritance rather than by anyone deciding it was right.

The question is not whether 0.5 is a good value. It is that a screen at a single clip scores every hand at one point on an axis that turns out to dominate the result.

Three of the plans the bench has been running sit outside their own band. g12, g23 and rv04_mid at 0.50 rad are all below theirs and drop every repeat. g12w11 at 1.10 is above g12's upper edge of 1.05 and holds one in four.

The isolated block at 0.40 is not an operating point. Several designs keep the tool there with 0/4 immediately above. A turn small enough not to disturb the grip is not a turn — the band is always the longest contiguous run.

The bench

The hand performs between 44% and 90% of the turn it is commanded, and it is torque

An open-loop plan is exactly the claim that commanded equals achieved. The control station logs both sides of it — the command stream it sent, and servo telemetry before and after. Twenty runs of three of the newly promoted hands, read back off the CB1 the same evening, say the claim is wrong by a factor of two on the joint that matters.

The pip joints arrive and the yaw joints do not. Every pip sits at 1.00 ± 0.05. Yaw runs from 0.44 to 0.90 — and yaw is the joint the turn is built out of.

It is not a following error. The session swept commanded speed from 0.10× to 1.50×. sv1_u0100's middle yaw reads 0.56 at both ends. A shortfall invariant to how slowly you ask for it is not the servo lagging.

It is torque. The terminal load on middle yaw is 405–495 on sv1_u0060 and 900–915 on sv1_u0100, against an overload trip that fires near 800. One run sits at exactly 200.0 — the protective_torque = 20% plateau this program has met before. The joint that falls furthest short is the one that is stalled.

Every simulated ranking is in commanded units. The clip the hand executes is smaller than the clip in the plan, by a design-dependent factor. The +0.10 rad margin in the current ship rule was sized against 4–6° of yaw droop measured a day earlier; the real correction is an order of magnitude larger.
What this session cannot tell us — none of the twenty runs recorded a rotation angle

manual_score is null on all 20. By the bench protocol's own rule that means the session produced no result: a rotation angle, with a repeat count and a stated measurement method.

sv1_u0060 achieves the most of its commanded turn and has the best simulated alignment of the three. sv1_u0100 achieves the least, has a completely idle thumb, and was one of the two singled out as working well by eye. With no rotation measurement on any run, that comparison contains no data — which is an argument for the AprilTag vane instrument the bench suite already specifies, not an argument about the hands.

Method, and a caution about the CB1's clock

Per joint, over the whole turn:

achieved fraction = (measured after − measured before)
                    ÷ (commanded end − commanded start)

Joints commanded to move less than 2° are skipped — a ratio taken over a 1° command is noise. Regenerate with scripts/real_v1_bench_arrival.py --logs <session>/logs.

The CB1's clock runs about 14.5 hours behind. There is no RTC and no NTP on that subnet, so it counts from boot. Run filenames order the session correctly but do not date it.

The population

Re-screening 4,096 hands across the clip, with a gate for what the servos will accept

If one clip is not a screen, the fix is to sweep it. Every fitted grasp cell in the 4,096-hand population was re-run at five clips from 0.50 to 1.30 rad under the full retention gate — a 60 mm proof lift the object has to follow, then five free seconds with at most 10 mm of slip, under the SCS0009 torque caps.

The screen also carries something it never had: a servo-command gate. A trajectory the driver would refuse is not a candidate, however well it scores, and until today that was only discovered at export — four stages downstream.

Raising the clip does not help this maneuver. Fewer cells pass at every step above 0.50, and the servo cap blocks a steeply growing share — 5,227 of 5,696 cells at 1.30 rad. That is the opposite of what the deployed plans wanted, and the two are not in conflict: the population screen ends with a 60 mm proof lift and five free seconds, and a bigger turn excursion costs grip. The bench plans hold the tool on a post with a fixed palm, where a bigger turn is what produces the rotation. Different maneuvers have different clips. The mistake was never that 0.5 was the wrong number — it is that one number was used for both.

The sweep still finds hands the single-clip screen could not see. Of 269 morphologies that pass the gate at some clip, 39 pass at no other value than one the old screen never tried, and 125 do their best work away from 0.50.

What the confirmation left, and what did not transfer

Five nominal repeats plus a four-draw wrong-hand ensemble leave 119 of 269 morphologies, and 50 of those confirm at no clip the old screen ever tried. Twenty full-error draws each put two hands ahead of anything the single-clip screen found — sv1_u1939 at 0.70 rad (0.35 of the full-error distribution) and sv1_u2745 at 1.10 (0.30), against 0.25 for sv1_u2699, the previous best on this population. Both are at clips that were never run.

Sixteen went through export, servo range, gantry travel and clearance. Thirteen passed, and none failed on servo range — the new upstream gate doing its job. The three that failed, failed on clearance the way the roadmap warned: sv1_w3408 reads 17.3 mm in the rollout and +4.6 mm along the commanded chord.

Then the result that matters. Those sixteen were selected under the retention maneuver — proof lift, free hold. The bench runs the fixed-palm schedule with the tool on a post. Band-scanned there, eleven of the sixteen keep the tool at no clip at all, including both new leaders. A hand chosen for proof-lift retention is not a hand that rolls a tool resting on a post, and the two searches have been pulling in opposite directions the whole time.

Three survive both. Re-exported at their bench clip, re-gated and copied to the station, they take first, second and fifth in the ranking below — sv1_w2360_b075, sv1_u1364_b080, sv1_u0308_b050. They are the first hands to reach this bench from a search that varied the clip, and the first to have passed a servo-command gate before being ranked rather than after.

sv1_u1676 repeats sv1_w0116 exactly: a band of 1.05–2.00 sitting entirely above the largest clip its servos can be commanded to, 0.80.

Why the single-trial numbers are not the answer, and what is

Stage one is one rollout per cell per clip. On a contact solve that varies with the settle, that is a filter, not a measurement — and each hand gets twenty chances (four grasp cells × five clips) to produce its best number, so taking the maximum selects hard on noise. Single-trial cosines run up to 0.998; they will not survive.

The point of making stage one cheap is to afford stage two: five nominal repeats plus a four-draw wrong-hand ensemble at each survivor's chosen clip, then twenty full-error draws for what remains. That is the number to quote.

One more caution about comparing across this page: the population screen's cosine is measured after a 60 mm proof lift and five free seconds of hold, and the deployed plans' cosine after a fixed-palm hold with the tool on a post. They are different maneuvers and the numbers are not interchangeable.

A uniform budget-1.0 audit exported all four Sobol-128 finalists and declined to ship any of them. They are on the station because four more points on the clip axis is what a hardware/simulation disagreement needs, not because the audit was wrong.

What is still open

Three things this day did not settle

01  The achieved clip is unmeasured as a clip. We know the hand performs 0.44–0.90 of its commanded yaw travel, but an achieved-travel factor is not a clip: the clip binds different joints at different points of the ramp. Replaying each hardware log against its plan in simulation would give the achieved trajectory directly, and that is the thing to compare a band against.

02  Nothing models the servo bodies. The clearance gate measures modelled finger links and tips. The printed parts put a servo case and a bracket around exactly those, so a margin held between two proximal segments is worth less than the same margin between two tips. The gate now names the two bodies at the minimum so this can at least be reasoned about; measuring it is a printed part and a caliper.

03  The operating cell was never swept jointly with the clip. Pivot height, turn angle, straddle and thumb placement were all chosen at 0.5 rad. Only the clip has been re-tuned around them.

04  The population is screened on a maneuver the bench does not run. Eleven of the sixteen best hands under the proof-lift retention gate keep the tool at no clip on the fixed-palm schedule. Either the screen should run the schedule the hardware runs, or the hardware should run the proof lift. Continuing to rank on one and deploy the other is the expensive option.
