Eight things to reconcile before presenting

each one is a place where the PDF and the record disagree, or where a question is likely and the answer is not in the paper

read first
  1. Trial count and D1’s hold rate. The PDF reports 80 trials, 51 held, D1 9/10. The corrected analysis of 2026-09-01 (paper/transfer_corrections.tex, docs/experiments/20260901-real_v1-transfer-firstpass/NUMBERS_CHANGED.md) is 77 trials, 48 held, 28 dropped, 1 unresolved, with D1 at 6/7. The three extra D1 holds come from a 15-trial session of which the operator scored three, all holds; they enter D1’s alignment pool (nc = 9) and are excluded from its rate because they were retained on the outcome being measured. The PDF’s 9/10 counts them. The 6/7 version is the defensible one.
  2. “64 % success rate in reorientation” is the hold rate (51 of 80 held). The paper has no separate reorientation criterion; §3 gives one that the data supports and its caveat.
  3. The 6,629 “hardware-feasible” count was computed under rail limits that were later corrected. On 2026-08-30 the driver believed J1/J3/J5 travelled 56.2/56.0/54.1 mm; on 2026-09-01 all six axes were measured at their full nominal travel (110 mm, 60 mm). All 1,569 rejections are on the x axes by 3.8–5.9 mm (index x: 467 designs, up to 4.0 mm; middle x: 698 designs, up to 5.9 mm; thumb x: 520 designs, up to 3.8 mm). Under the corrected rails every one of the 8,198 fits. The rejected corner is the compact end of x, which is also the direction the retention screen favours, so the filter removed some of the population the screen would have kept. Say “6,629 under the travel envelope measured at the time”.
  4. The simulated prediction in Table IV is the bench-schedule replay, not the retention-screen score. Each “Sim c” is the mean held cosine over four replicates of the exported plan on a fixed palm with the tool on the 100 mm post and a 9.6 s hold (deploy_plan_bands_all.json). The retention screen that selected the population ranks the same hands differently (D3 leads it at 0.913; D1 leads the replay at 0.827). Both are in the table in §4.
  5. D4’s peak substitution is the most favourable value it could take. Over every value D4’s bench cosine could hold, ρs ranges 0.36–0.55; the substituted 0.819 gives 0.50, and dropping D4 gives 0.54 on seven hands.
  6. The eight hands are a concentrated set. Median pairwise mount separation 43.6 mm against 66.9 mm for eight random members of the population, below the first percentile of 20,000 random eights; seven of eight sit in the compact quadrant. A rank correlation over them is a statement about a corner of the design space.
  7. Table I’s yaw range is the servo’s. ±85° is the measured range; the live command cap is ±70° (manta_hand/plan.py, item 4), and one promoted design (sv1_w0116) was undeployable because its turn needed −84° of middle yaw.
  8. “Up to 110 Hz control” is the read rate. Nine position reads run at 111 Hz after the FTDI latency timer is set to 1 ms; a write-plus-nine-reads loop runs at 75 Hz; the deployed policy stream is write-only by design (no servo reads during a stream), so a residual policy on the current path would see the AprilTag pose and its own commands and nothing from the servos.

The design space is six numbers

A configuration is the palm-frame (x, y) of each finger mount: thumb inside a 60 × 110 mm box, index and middle inside 60 × 60 mm boxes, all centred on the CAD-nominal mounts at (−50, 0), (50, 55) and (50, −55) mm. The fingers are identical CAD parts (links 33.45 / 33.45 / 37.16 mm axis-to-surface, capsule radius 10.55 mm, joints overlapping 12.70 mm), so nothing else varies and a sampled hand is realised by driving three gantries. The earlier simulation program used nine parameters, the ninth per finger being a proximal-length slide; REAL_V1_WORKSPACE pins those three to zero because the hardware has no such joint. All six slides at zero is the CAD-nominal hand, which is also the widest tripod the machine offers.

Stage A: the population (8,198)

The population is a scrambled Sobol sequence in seven dimensions (scipy.stats.qmc.Sobol(d=7, scramble=True, seed=20260830)). Six coordinates map to the six mounts. The seventh is a stratum selector: points with u7 < 0.25 have their index-x and middle-x coordinates transformed by u → u0.75, which shifts them toward the outward end of their travel. That stratum encodes a packaging intuition about wider finger pairs without letting the intuition remove coverage: three quarters of the population stays uniform. Tags carry the stratum (sv1_u uniform, sv1_w biased). Two properties decided the sampler:

  • Prefix stability. The first N points of a longer draw are exactly the shorter draw, so the 128-point pilot, the 4,096 screen and the 8,192 screen are nested and a design keeps its tag and its generated scene across passes. Only the new half of each pass costs anything. D5 (sv1_u0060) was promoted from the 128-point prefix and is the same point in the full draw.
  • Low discrepancy inside the selector. Keeping the stratum choice in the Sobol sequence keeps the 25 % mix even at every prefix, which a coin flip per point would not.

Six anchor hands are added to every population so a new pass can be read against the existing evidence: rv00_wide, rv01_compact, rv03_narrowy, rv04_mid (the 2 × 2 factorial plus centre of the first real_v1 study), rv05_manual (the hand-authored design) and g12 (the first design with a collision-safe exported plan). 8,192 + 6 = 8,198.

Stage A2: the rails (6,629)

Each design’s three mounts are checked against the gantry travel with the same mount_violations call the deployment plan uses, computed from the design vector rather than the manifest’s rounded mounts (seven designs sit within 0.05 mm of a limit, where rounding flips the verdict). The travel envelope in force on 2026-08-30 was short on three axes, which is the caveat in item 3; rv01_compact is among the 1,569 rejected.

Stage B: the grasp fit (3,298 designs, 11,444 rows)

fit_real_v1_pose.py solves, in closed form and then by IK, a palm height and nine finger angles that put three pads on a 25 mm shaft lying on a 100 mm post: the index and middle pads straddle the thumb along the shaft by a straddle distance, the thumb sits an axial offset along the shaft from their midpoint, and every pad lands at or below the shaft’s equator (a pad above the equator wedges the shaft out; measured on 2026-09-06, the tool rises and escapes in 0.6 s of standing still). The fit is run at four cells: straddle 32 or 40 mm × thumb axial offset 10 or 20 mm, with the grip depth searched automatically and a 4 mm commanded squeeze. A design admits a pose in a cell when all three pads reach their targets inside joint limits without the MCP hitting its −15° stop; compact hands fail because the pads cannot ring the shaft, wide hands because the pair cannot reach across it. 3,298 of 6,629 admit at least one cell; rows by cell: straddle 32 mm, thumb 10 mm: 2,894, straddle 32 mm, thumb 20 mm: 2,620, straddle 40 mm, thumb 10 mm: 3,138, straddle 40 mm, thumb 20 mm: 2,792. The thumb offset is in the cell grid because on the earlier 108-hand search it was the single largest lever: sliding the thumb 20 mm along the shaft took rv04_mid from held cos 0.000 (0/8 kept) to 0.972 (8/8), by giving the thumb’s normal force a moment arm about the pinch axis.

Stage D: the retention screen (535)

Every (design, cell) row is executed once at each of five residual clips, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.1 and 1.3 rad, for 57,220 rollouts on CPU MuJoCo (2 ms steps, 18 workers, about an hour). One rollout is the maneuver the bench runs:

  1. The tool stands on a 100 mm post at y = −35 mm; the palm is fixed; the pads are the built hand’s flat 14.8 × 21.1 mm faces (--flat-pads), since the earlier round-capsule model made point contact and mis-sized every grasp by 2–5 mm.
  2. The fingers close to the fitted grip with a 10 mm commanded squeeze, then a 400-step capture (0.8 s) under a proprioceptive load regulator (target 250 load units, the bench’s per-finger relief) and the servo’s turn-phase torque ceiling of 0.180 N·m (80 % of the SCS0009 stall torque, where its overload protection fires).
  3. The turn: the grasp anchor is rotated by −80° about an axis parallel to the pinch axis and raised above the contact plane by axis_k = 0.15 of the half-straddle (5–6 mm), per-finger IK maps the moving pad targets to joints, and a straight line in joint space from the anchor to that end pose is played over 550 steps (1.1 s). Each joint’s excursion from the anchor is clipped to ±clip. The torque ceiling then drops to the rated 0.069 N·m for the hold.
  4. A proof lift: the palm rises 60 mm over 700 steps (1.4 s). Only a captured tool follows; a tool resting on a finger or the post does not.
  5. A 2,500-step hold (5.0 s), during which the tool may slip at most 10 mm.

The rollout is retained (ok) when at least one hand contact remains, the tool’s minimum height stays within 20 mm of its lifted height, it never touches the post, the proof lift raised it at least 48 mm (80 % of the commanded 60), and it slipped at most 10 mm through the hold. A (cell, clip) row passes when it is retained, its final signed cosine to vertical is at least 0.7 (within 45.6° of vertical), the index–middle clearance along the turn is at least 5 mm, and no commanded joint exceeds the servo’s measured range. A design passes when any of its rows does. 535 pass; 460 of them pass at 0.5 rad and 75 only at another clip (selected clips: 0.5 rad: 305, 0.7 rad: 123, 0.9 rad: 39, 1.1 rad: 35, 1.3 rad: 33).

Why the clip is swept: every screen before 2026-08-30 planned the turn at 0.5 rad, a number inherited from the reinforcement-learning residual’s action budget, where it means something else. Re-screening the Sobol-4096 population across five clips showed each hand keeps the tool only inside a contiguous band of clips and drops it on both sides, that alignment is not monotone inside the band, and that a single-clip screen leaves 14 % of the passing population unseen. Why one trial per row: a single trial on a contact solver is a filter, not a measurement, and its job here is to be cheap enough to run 57,220 times; the confirmation spends the saved compute on the survivors.

Stage 3: confirmation (227)

Each of the 535 designs is re-run at its own selected (cell, clip): five nominal replicates (0.5 mm spawn jitter) and four draws from the half-error ensemble described in §5. A design confirms when at least three of the five nominal replicates are retained, the mean held cosine (a released replicate scoring 0) is at least 0.7, and the clearance and servo gates still pass. 227 confirm; 138 at 0.5 rad (0.5 rad: 138, 0.7 rad: 47, 0.9 rad: 13, 1.1 rad: 16, 1.3 rad: 13). Stage 4 then draws twenty full-error rollouts per confirmed design (227 cells); its outputs, ens_kept and ens_win (retained with cosine ≥ 0.7), rank robustness and are what the promotion reads.

From 227 to eight

Promotion is a separate, hand-triggered step per pass. The leaders of each pass by full-error win are exported (real_v1_export_plan.py: a plan of three set-points with the clip, a 50 Hz per-joint trajectory CSV, a build sheet and a pose sheet) and run through four gates: every commanded joint inside the servo’s measured range (with the ±70° yaw cap); every mount inside the gantry travel; index–middle clearance at least 5 mm along both the three-set-point chord the station interpolates and the CSV path (they are different paths, and a plan can clear one and not the other); and plan validity. The survivors are then band-scanned on the bench schedule (33 clips × 4 replicates, 9.6 s hold) and shipped at a clip inside the band, at least 0.10 rad above its lower edge to absorb the measured yaw droop, with the best alignment among the clips the servos can be told. The retention gate and the bench schedule keep disagreeing about which hands work: of the five gate-passers promoted from the 8,192 pass, two had a bench band (sv1_w6689, sv1_u7952); of sixteen from the 4,096 re-screen, eleven kept the tool at no clip on the bench schedule. Nineteen plans reached the station across all passes.

The eight bench hands are the top eight distinct morphologies among the sixteen exported, clearance-safe plans, ordered by the bench-schedule replay (docs/experiments/20260831-real_v1-transfer-protocol/README.md, §3). Six are Sobol members (D1, D2, D3, D5, D6, D8), D4 is the g12 anchor and D7 is the manual hand. Three exported plans were excluded before the study by the clearance gate: g23 (0.8 mm), rv04_mid (−2.6 mm) and g24 (−5.2 mm; the simulator scores it 0.750, which would have placed it second).

What the funnel selects, and what it never scored

Selection moves toward compact tripods. In the plane of thumb-to-pair span and index–middle opening (both linear in the six mounts), the quadrant below the population medians holds 25 % of the reachable population, 65 % of the retained, 71 % of the confirmed and seven of the eight deployed; the screen cuts the spread of those two coordinates to 0.59 and 0.74 of the population’s and leaves the pair’s x offset and y midpoint alone. Principal components of the retained 535 carry 47/21/17 % on three directions and 8/4/2 % on the rest, so the survivors lie on a roughly three-dimensional sheet inside the six-dimensional box. The depth of the grasp fit separates retained from discarded designs at AUC 0.80 and predicts alignment not at all (ρs +0.08 over the 535); it is a gate on whether a hand works, not a ranking among the hands that do. The KaRMA rolling-pinch metric behaves the same way on this family (AUC 0.80 for retention, ρs 0.11 against the screened turn).

The funnel trained no policy and scored one maneuver. The retention gate’s proof lift is a different maneuver from the bench’s fixed-palm hold, and the two order hands differently; the ranking the paper tests is the bench schedule’s. The simulator retained the tool on all four replicates of seven of the eight deployed plans, so its retention prediction carries no information about the bench’s 2/10–10/10.

The simulator and the bench disagree in one convention. The simulator’s held cosine counts a release as zero, so it is a retention-weighted alignment. The bench cosine averages the trials that held. The pre-registered protocol asked for parity (“a bench trial that released the tool contributes cos_up = 0”); the analysis went the other way because the tag cannot tell a turn from a fall, so a dropped trial has no honest alignment to contribute. The two conventions give different rankings, in §4.

A success criterion the data supports

Forty-four of the 48 held trials finish between 20° and 60° of net turn, against 4 of the 28 drops. The drops leave that band in both directions: 12 pass 60°, 12 stop short of 20°. The proposal from the record (project_eight_hand_bench_record): a trial is a reorientation when it is held, its net turn is inside 20–60°, and its slip is under 20 mm (held slips run 4.9–10.9 mm; ejections 24–54 mm). Under that rule the eight hands score 44/77 rather than 51/80, and every count stays attributable to a trial.

The caveat belongs in the same sentence: restricting each trial to rotation recorded while the cylinder was still rising moves the drop side of the contingency from 4/28 to 17/28. Most drops turn as far as the holds before they let go; the 20–60° band separates outcomes after the fact more than it describes a mechanism. What the tag cannot supply is a contact signal, and the transfer sessions logged no servo load (fixed at the source for later sessions: --telemetry-hz 10 --servo-fields load). The two drop modes that survive the correction are established by lateral travel and by a shortfall in rotation, not by the peak: D6’s seven drops travel 24–54 mm with no net turn (ejection), and D3’s five drops travel like a hold and stop at 25° against its holds’ 42° (stall, p = 0.040). “Overshoot” was withdrawn: D8’s held and dropped trials are identical to 1.4 s, then the holds finish at 46–59°/s and stop at 52° while the drops rotate at 166–217°/s with the centre descending at about half of g. The extra 20° is the shaft being flung.

A rotation threshold that the simulator and the bench could share is c ≥ 0.7 on a held trial. Against the corrected per-hand means, D1 (0.834), D2 (0.797), D4 (0.819, peak), D5 (0.938) and D8 (0.773) sit above it and D3 (0.689), D6 (0.614) and D7 (0.553) below; D7, the hand that retains every trial, is 0.15 short of it with sd 0.049. A per-trial count under this rule needs the corrected per-trial table regenerated: data.json in the first-pass folder predates the 2026-09-01 corrections, and real_v1_transfer_figures.py currently fails on D4’s void hold window when asked to rebuild it.

What scoring on held trials does to the ranking

The alignment column is conditioned on survival, and the survival fraction runs from 0.2 to 1.0 across the eight. D5’s 0.938 is the mean of the three trials that held; its seven drops turned to 73° on average and left. D6’s 0.614 rests on two trials. The simulator’s convention scores a release as zero, which produces a retention-weighted alignment: D1 0.715, D2 0.797, D3 0.275, D4 0.737, D5 0.281, D6 0.123, D7 0.553, D8 0.309. Against the simulated column that gives ρs = 0.43 (p = 0.29) instead of 0.50, and D5 moves from first to sixth. Neither convention is wrong; they answer different questions. Held-only alignment asks how far a hand turns the tool when it keeps it, which is the quantity the geometric plan predicts. Retention-weighted alignment asks how often a trial ends with a turned, held tool, which is what a downstream task needs. The paper reports the first and should say so, and report the second beside it.

The conditioning also explains the near-zero correlation between hold rate and alignment (ρs = −0.08): the hands that turn furthest when they hold are the ones that most often do not hold (D5, D8), and the hand that always holds turns least (D7). Retention and alignment are two outcomes of one open-loop plan, and the plan trades them: in the simulated chain, past about 59° of turn no cell carries the tool and every cell that turns 44° or less carries 4/4 (docs/experiments/20260906-screened_turn/). The mechanism is in the simulator’s own per-finger trace: through the turn the index pad leaves at about half of it and the total pad force falls from 28 N to 0.3 N by 70 %, so the last third of the rotation is the tool settling into vertical in a hand that is barely holding it.

The D4 peak substitution

On every one of D4’s nine held trials the vane leaves the decodable image near vertical, so its hold-window cosine is void and its peak stands in. Peak and hold alignment agree in rank (ρs = 0.83 over the eight; r = 0.82 and ρs = 0.86 over the 52 complete traces) and the peak is always the larger number, with a residual sd of 0.103 between them. The substituted 0.819 is therefore an upper bound on D4’s true value. Two sensitivity checks: removing D4 gives ρs = 0.54 on seven hands; replacing its value with any number from 0 to 1 gives ρs between 0.36 and 0.55, so the substitution can move the headline coefficient by at most 0.14 and the reported value sits at the top of that range. The fix is instrumental: a vane with the tag 50 mm from the centre instead of 77 mm cuts the swept arc by a third and keeps the 40 mm tag’s decode margin.

Eight hands

The eight-point coefficient 0.50 has p = 0.21 and a bootstrap 95 % interval over hands of [−0.29, 1.00]; the two-sided 5 % critical value at n = 8 is 0.74. The pre-registered protocol (n = 16 plans) stated the critical value of 0.50 for its design and called itself powered for a strong correlation and underpowered for a weak one; the study ran eight morphologies at ten trials rather than sixteen plans at four. The secondary correlations are descriptive at this n: contact force 0.64 (p = 0.09), contact count −0.52, and nothing simulated orders hold rate (−0.29 ≤ ρs ≤ 0.27). Two further reasons to read them as hypotheses rather than results:

  • Within-eight correlations inherit the selection. The eight were chosen in simulated-alignment order, so any variable correlated with the selection correlates with alignment inside the set. The depth of the grasp fit reads ρs = −0.90 (p = 0.002) against simulated cosine over the eight and +0.076 over the 535 retained designs. Check any within-eight coefficient against the population before believing it.
  • The set is narrow. Median pairwise mount separation 43.6 mm (minimum 14.4 mm, D4–D8), below the first percentile of random eights from the population and the third percentile from the 535; the six Sobol members alone are at the 0.1st percentile. The coefficient describes one corner of the box.

The plant correction of 2026-09-02 (finger gain 30 → 0.5, the calibrated servo) reproduces one hand’s level (rv05: retention 0.94 against the bench’s 0.92, cosine 0.638 against 0.593) and does not repair the ordering: replaying the exported trajectories on the corrected plant moves ρs from −0.54 to +0.11 over seven hands in that harness. Both of its largest misses are grip cases in opposite directions (D3 slips on the bench and never in the sim; D8 drops in the sim on every trial and on the bench on some). What the simulator lacks is contact, and identifying contact needs the bench.

Morphology positioning

The six gantries run on T5 × 1 lead screws at 3,216 steps/mm, homed sensorlessly against a hard stop by TMC5160 StallGuard2 with a timeout fallback sized to cover the full travel (J3 and J5 do not trip StallGuard at the current tuning and home on the timeout every time, which is still a hard-stop home). A 45 mm caliper check on J1/J3/J5 found 0.0 % scale error (verify_frame_mapping.py --travel), and the station adopts an existing home when the gantries sit within 0.5 mm of a plan’s targets. No dial-indicator repeatability of the home itself is on record; that measurement (home ten times, read one mount against a fixed indicator) is a half-hour item and should be reported as a number rather than an assumption. Two sim-side facts bound its importance: the deployment envelope’s mount-error axis at 2 mm changes retention through the turn from 77 % to 80 % pooled over 18 designs (placement and servo zeros matter far more), and the nearest pair of deployed hands is 14.4 mm apart, so a sub-millimetre home does not confuse one design with another.

Trial-to-trial repeatability

Stage 1 of the transfer protocol ran twelve consecutive trials of one plan (sv1_w6689_b060, D1) with a full re-stage between each (open, remove the tool, re-seat it). Seven carried a hold-window cosine: mean +0.881, sd 0.011, 95 % interval on the sd [0.007, 0.024] (docs/experiments/20260831-real_v1-transfer-protocol/sessions/20260831-1845-sv1_w6689-stage1/README.md). Five lost the vane tag at 1.4–2.4 s and carried no cosine, which is what led to the 40 mm tag reprint. Against the simulated between-plan spread of 0.24 that is a 22:1 ratio: four trials per plan resolve a design difference of 0.02. In the study itself D1’s own session had sd 0.032 over six holds; adding the three holds from the later session tripled it to 0.092 (turns of 41, 55 and 72° from one plan), so re-staging is the variance. Session-to-session drift on the same plan and the same tag is ±5° (u0060 +4.0, u0308 +4.8, w0099 −4.5° between sessions), and w6689 moved 14.5° between two consecutive sessions in the 30 mm-tag era. Within-design sd for six of the seven measurable designs is 0.027–0.061 against a between-design spread of 0.49–0.94, so the bench separates the hands; D3 is the exception at 0.221 because its trials are a mixture of held grips and partial slips (0.21, 0.30, 0.32, 0.33, 0.61, 0.66, 0.70, 0.78).

The instrument

Two AprilTags: a 40 mm static tag as the world-up datum and a 30 mm (later 40 mm) tag on a vane 71 mm (later 77 mm) along the shaft, read by a RealSense D435 at 30 fps. Noise floor against a static target: 0.017° rms in angle, 0.030 mm rms in height. The operator’s by-eye estimate was ±5° and could not see axial slip; two earlier results were traced to it (a “90°” that was the shaft falling through the grasp). The vane is placed on the end of the shaft that rises, normal to the pinch axis, so the turn is an in-plane image rotation.

The learned controllers, and why the paper deploys a derived one

what was trained, what it established, the reasons it is not on the bench, and where it stands on 2026-09-20 · the pipeline as it runs today, with a decision register, is on the robust-tranche page (local · published)

What was trained

The task is the paper’s: a 25 mm × 100 mm cylinder lying on the floor is closed on, lifted 100 mm, and turned to vertical by the nine finger joints alone. The policy is a residual: at 50 Hz it emits nine numbers, each scaled by 0.5 rad and added to a scripted finger set-point that eases from the open keyframe to the fitted grip over the first 80 physics steps; the six palm joints follow a scripted lift and are never learned. Action zero reproduces the open-loop grasp and lift, which is the property that lets the same recipe run on any hand with a fitted grip. The actor is an MLP 512-256-128 reading 66 numbers: 15 joint positions and 15 velocities, the tool position (3) and pose (7) in the palm frame, the finger joints (9) and tool pose (7) of a reference rollout of the same grip at this step, the previous action (9), and 1 − cos between the shaft and vertical. PPO (rsl_rl) on 3,072 copies of the frozen scene in MuJoCo-Warp through mjlab, 24 steps per iteration, 20 M steps in about 36 min on the workstation’s RTX 4070 Ti SUPER; the GPU is saturated at about 10,000 steps/s and the environment count buys nothing.

The reward that converged, in order of weight: a progress term (300 × the per-step change in cosine), an alignment term (100 × exp(−4(1 − cos)²)), a lift term during the lift, contact terms (all three pads on the tool, mean pad contact), tracking terms against the reference rollout, and penalties on lateral drift, finger drift from the grip, action rate, joint limits and pad force above 6 N. Both reorientation terms switch on at policy step 58, half a second after the lift ramp ends, together with the tip-lost termination (fewer than three pads for 15 consecutive steps). The episode is 5 s. The recipe is configs/recipes/b_liveA.yaml plus the run’s own flags.

The architecture came from a seam. A single lift-and-turn policy could not be trained: a warm start from a lift policy carried a hold-still prior that PPO never overrode, and from scratch the rewards fought. The fix was two policies, A for the lift and B for the turn, which created a new problem at the hand-off: a B trained from a teleported pre-lifted spawn drops A’s organic delivery (post-handoff minimum height 0.002–0.012 m across every injection and co-adaptation variant tried, June 2–9). The live-A reset closed it on 2026-06-10: a frozen A drives the first 40 steps of every B episode in real physics and those steps are masked from the PPO update, so B sees the delivery it will get. On the real_v1 hand the lift is scripted (the fitter’s grip plus the palm ramp), so there is no A, and the “live-A” recipe runs with the script in A’s place.

What the policies established

  • The turn is achievable in-hand on the earlier m05 hand: b33 holds the continuous hand-off at cos 0.891 (peak 0.99) on three loaded pads; a warm-started reorient draw has a seed sd of 0.032 across 16 draws.
  • Their terminal poses and the shape of the finger motion are what the geometric plan reproduces. The hold_target_ctrl anchor lerp that became the deployed controller was written for the opposed-pair hand as the policy’s set-point schedule; the ±0.5 rad clip that every screen inherited was the policy’s action budget.
  • They defined the evaluation discipline the bench work then used: score the deterministic held cosine, never the reward; look at the rollout before explaining its numbers; a held peak is not a hold; evaluate as a distribution.

Why the paper deploys the derived controller

Each reason is a measurement, and together they order the work that followed.

  1. A trained controller’s quality varied with the draw more than with the hand. From-scratch reorient training on one design spans held cos −0.29 to 0.78 across seeds (m05, n = 5, sd 0.38); a local design’s apparent lead of 0.07 sat inside pooled noise of 0.41. Seventy GPU-hours of probes (July 10–13) showed pick-up failures were draw noise 5/5, that single draws are not measurements, and that no property of the delivered grasp predicted the turn (n = 50). Comparing hands needs a controller whose quality does not depend on the hand; a derived plan applies the identical computation to every morphology.
  2. The reference policy ignored its observations. Replaying another environment’s values over the whole 66-dimensional input leaves b33’s held cosine at 0.900 against 0.895 (2026-08-30; reproduced on the real_v1 geometry by an independent implementation). It is a learned open-loop residual indexed by step, so a closed-loop deployment would carry no closed-loop content, and its 5 mm / 5° spawn sensitivity (hold 0.97 → 0.41) is what an open-loop plan has anyway.
  3. Most of the learned turn was floor work. Re-expressed in the object frame, 69 % of b33’s alignment gain and 46 % of the opposed hand’s occurs while the shaft still touches the floor, and the wrist contributes 0 %. The first floor-free turn in the program was the raised-axis geometric plan of 2026-08-27.
  4. The rolling turn depends on the contact model. Hardening the contact (solimp 0.97/0.995 → 0.985/0.999) leaves the grasp intact and collapses the turn (alignment 13 against 48); pad friction ×0.5 drops the hold to 0.09, a 15 % thinner shaft to 0.03, a stiffer contact the reorient to 0.09. Compliance domain randomisation relocated the policy to the middle of its band instead of broadening it (July 8–10, and again August 18 with friction and compliance arms). The behaviour was a narrow-band contact behaviour.
  5. The topology changed. Every policy through August 26 was trained on m05, a 117 mm finger with coincident yaw and MCP axes and a proximal-length slide. The CAD hand of 2026-08-27 has 33/33/37 mm links, a 32 mm yaw link and no length joint; b33 reaches peak cos 0.019 on it. Four fresh real_v1 A→B pipelines held the hand-off and turned the shaft 0.9–4.0°, because the fitted grasp was a rotational lock (the turn was paid for in finger extension, of which 1–7 mm remained). The lock was found and the axis raised in the open-loop probe, not by training.
  6. The hardware could not supply the observation. On 2026-08-29 the platform’s feedback was nine joint positions at 6.95 Hz (the FTDI latency timer), no object pose, no contact and no current register; the policy needed 66 inputs at 50 Hz including the tool pose. The bus was brought to 111 Hz and the AprilTag pair added on 08-30/31, after the plans were exported; the deployed stream is still write-only.
  7. The simulator’s actuator was 60× too stiff. The scenes shipped with finger kp 30; the bench calibrates to about 0.5. At kp 30 a commanded angle is an achieved angle and the finger-driven 90° turn exists; at the calibrated gain the fingers move the tool 0–10° through the same plan and the bench’s 20–50° tip is what the calibrated plant reproduces (cos 0.64 against 0.59 on rv05). Every policy before 2026-09-16 trained on the stiff plant, and every one before 2026-09-20 on an object with MuJoCo’s default contact parameters and mjlab’s default floor rather than the scene’s.
  8. Unbounded residuals were unphysical. The D6 finetunes of September 17 emit |a| > 1 on 73 % of steps and command joints up to 384° beyond their range; a real servo would sit on its overload cliff. Bounding the residual at ±1 rad (September 19) is what made a policy that a plan could carry.

Where it stands on 2026-09-20

Policies are now trained on the calibrated plant (kp 0.5, kv 0.02, 0.35 N·m, elliptic cone, the built +25 mm palm plate, the screw-tip tool, the scene’s floor and contact class) with the object pose observed. D6 from scratch at 60 M steps holds 64/64 at cos 0.933 with the tool rolling across the pads from step 68; a 20 M grip finetune reaches 0.969 at 10.8 N (page). Warm-started from it, sixteen jobs across D1–D7 with the residual bounded at ±1 rad hold 64/64 at the trained pose on every hand but one draw (D6 seed 0, 56/64), while the separation term kills the turn on D4 and D7; evaluated with the tool spawned 3 mm and 10° off, D7 holds 0/64 and D5 26/64, because training had spawned the tool at one pose (page). Continued with spawn jitter, ten of twelve jobs trained (D4 NaN twice on the mesh tool); the jittered hold ranking is D5 57/64, D5 with separation 56, D1 with separation 54 (cos 0.958, the tranche’s best turn), D6 with separation 51, D3 50, D6 49, D2 48, D1 47, D7 35. Not built: the bench controller that would feed a policy the tag pose and stream its commands at 50 Hz; the blind actor with a privileged critic; a seeded trainer (--seed is parsed and never applied). Nothing learned has run on the hand.

Future work, expounded

the six items of the Discussion and the five of Appendix D, each with what it means on this platform, what the record already says about it, the first measurement, and how it sits against Yang et al. (RA-L 2024; TEVC 2026) · everything here is a proposal

Yang et al. built a reconfigurable hand with 37 hardware parameters set by hand, pre-trained a grasping policy with those parameters randomised, then optimised the parameters along “hardware gradients” of a force-closure fitness over 20 YCB objects, and in the TEVC paper ran population evolution with morphology-aware learning across three-, four- and five-fingered hands, transferring evolved designs to hardware. Two things distinguish what this program can add: the morphology is motor-driven, so a design is a 95 s gantry move and dense physical traversal is cheap; and the object of study is the sim-to-real error Δ(m) as a function over the design space, which their work does not measure. The proposals below follow from that.

1. Automating environment and evaluation reset

Every one of the 251 archived trials was staged by hand: open the hand, remove the tool, re-seat it on the post, centre it by eye. Stage 1 showed re-staging is the variance (sd 0.011 within a session, ±5° between sessions), and the deployment envelope says the tight axis is 2–6 mm along the tool. An automated reset is a fixture that returns the cylinder to a repeatable pose (a V-block on the post with a stop at the shaft end), plus the UR5e doing the re-stage, which the chain work already runs in simulation. The measurement that says it worked: stage 1 again with the fixture, and the between-session drift with it. The number to beat is sd 0.011 and ±5°. This item gates everything with “many trials” in it.

2. Slop, state estimation, stiffness and actuator torque

The servo is the largest sim-to-real term on record: a 0.018° per load-unit spring, a protection cliff at 80 % of stall torque that fires on 42 % of middle-yaw runs, and no current register. The consequence in the simulator is that the finger-driven 90° turn exists only above a gain of about 2 and the calibrated hand tips the tool 20–50°. The options in order of cost: raise protective_torque (a writable register, with the independent 70 °C thermal cutoff kept); a stiffer servo in the yaw position only, since that joint carries the turn; a host-side integral term on achieved angle, which the 2026-09-20 measurement showed cannot help while the servo sits on its ceiling. The measurement: the free-air and loaded arrival tables (real_v1_bench_arrival.py) before and after, on one hand. A larger payload and “all degrees of freedom for control” both need the torque first; the gantries at 3 mm/s cannot contribute to a turn, so using them in control means using them between trials.

3. Actuator system identification, and residual RL to stabilise the open-loop plan

The sysid is done for the plant’s stiffness and cliff and not for its speed: kv was never measured (the calibration’s deficit halves between kv 0.6 and 0.02) and the fitted gain is known to a factor of two (0.25–0.5). A free-air 20° step per joint at 111 Hz gives the time constant and the no-load speed in an afternoon. The residual is the other half: the six bench drops all turn the shaft to a mean peak of +0.61 and then let go, which is the failure a residual is for, since it needs to notice the shaft escaping rather than discover the turn. The record says what the residual must be: bounded (±1 rad), trained on the calibrated plant with spawn jitter, sighted through the tag pose, warm-started from a checkpoint that already turns, and gated at deployment on its commanded envelope rather than on a fixed path. What is missing is the station loop that feeds it. The first hardware test is one hand (D5 or D6), the policy’s own grasp, ten trials, against the same hand’s open-loop plan in the same sitting.

4. Sensorised, co-designed fingertips and palms

The fingertip result is quantitative in two directions that disagree: the rolling turn wants a compact convex point contact of 5–6 mm radius with 1–3 mm of deflection at load, and the gait wants a 13 mm radius because the pad ring is a gear. The flat built pad’s line contact is more tolerant to placement than the round model (8 mm axial bands against 1–2). A quick-release tip is already on the hand, so the experiment is a set of printed tips (r 5, 8, 13 mm; two durometers) run through the same ten-trial protocol on two hands. Sensing on the tip closes the loop the servo cannot (no current register): a force-sensitive resistor or a barometric pad per tip supplies the contact signal the transfer sessions lacked, and it is what turns the 20–60° band from a post-hoc separator into a stopping condition. The palm is a separate question: every trained policy held the object 7–8 cm below the palm with zero palm force, and the basketball study found the +25 mm plate blocks seating; a palm that the object can rest against changes the grasp class before it changes the sensing.

5. A motorised proximal-phalange length

The simulation evidence is mixed and honest about why. The nine-parameter sweeps found the length axis contributes mostly downside variance (freezing it raised the floor from −0.39 to −0.11 and the peak from 0.48 to 0.75 without touching the draw noise); the sp25 hand gripped better than m05 and its turn was unresolved at n = 1; the link-length gate found that with the 32 mm yaw link all candidate lengths clear the geometric ceiling, so only a controller can rank them; and the reorientation primitive found kinematic feasibility does not discriminate designs. On the hardware, a telescoping proximal adds one stepper per finger to a board that has room for two more. The measurement before building it is in simulation and cheap: the Sobol funnel with a seventh design coordinate (length, 25–45 mm), run through the same retention screen, to see whether the retained sheet moves along it. If the 535 do not separate on length under the open-loop plan, the DoF is not worth a motor for this task.

6. Fast, universal, morphology-adaptive control representations

The concrete form this takes here is a design-conditioned policy: one actor and critic trained across per-environment randomised mounts with the six-vector in the observation. The feasibility spike found MuJoCo-Warp already indexes model fields per world and mjlab expands them, so it is plumbing rather than engine work. It pays four ways: per-design evaluation becomes a rollout (the July program spent about 3 h of GPU per design draw and could not resolve differences under 0.25); the multi-hand residual for item 3 falls out; the frozen critic is a differentiable design surrogate in the manner of Bohlinger and Peters’ value-gradient search, with no differentiable simulator; and the critic’s gradient can be checked against the thumb-moment-arm predictor found by search. The one ablation that gates all of it is whether the behaviour changes when only the design vector changes, since b33 ignored its inputs entirely. Conditioning is not ranking: a policy that controls many hands never has to say which hand is better, so it is not blocked by the ranking result, and it is the item that makes Yang et al.’s “meta policy” comparable on this platform.

Appendix D (i): local and global sim-to-real agreement

The global test is the n = 8 study. The local test was designed and never run: the station holds g12 at four clips, u0060 at two and u0100 at two, so within a family the hand is identical and only the controller moves. If the bench does not reproduce a within-family ordering, a cross-family ordering means nothing, and the protocol says to answer that first. Twenty trials, one sitting. The geometric analogue is two hands one gantry move apart (D4–D8 are 14.4 mm apart), which asks whether Δ(m) is smooth at the scale the search would step.

Appendix D (ii): sparse hardware queries correcting a large simulated ranking

This is the bi-level plan’s Eq. 3, and the record contains one attempt at the inner step: correct the plant from bench data and re-rank. It reproduced one hand’s level and not the ordering, and the reason is that the missing physics is contact, which the servo sysid does not touch. A correction fitted to eight numbers cannot be validated on the same eight. The honest version of this item is sequential: choose the next hands to test where the simulator’s two scores (retention-screen and bench-schedule) disagree most, since those are the points that discriminate between candidate corrections, and keep the eight as the held-out set. The first three such hands are in docs/experiments/20260904-real_v1_ranked/ (the settle’s sign flips between w5120 and u5860).

Appendix D (iii): warm-started controllers as morphology changes

In simulation the effect is measured: a from-scratch reorient draw has sd 0.3–0.5 in held cosine, a warm-started one 0.032, and the September tranches warm-started every hand from one D6 checkpoint and reached 0.90–0.96 on six of seven. What is not measured is whether a warm-started controller ranks hands the same way an independently optimised one does, and the record has a warning: the shared warm start carries its source hand’s grip into every design (b33 is m05’s own), so the design-neutral object-frame imitation prior was needed to make the comparison fair. On hardware this item is the cheapest of the appendix: the gantries move between trials, the policy stays loaded, and the question is how many trials on the new hand it takes before the held rate matches the per-hand policy.

Appendix D (iv): task-conditioned specialisation on hardware

One task and one object have been run on hardware. The funnel selects compact tripods for the cylinder; the basketball study selects the widest layout the gantries offer (every axis at its travel end) and finds the eight bench hands cannot reach a size-7 ball at all. That is a specialisation the platform can show directly, since the two designs are one morphology change apart. The proposed measurement is two objects (the cylinder and a 90–120 mm ball) on the two layouts, ten trials each, with retention as the outcome; the simulation prediction is on the basketball page and the pad friction on a real ball is the unmeasured number.

Appendix D (v): morphologies that trade nominal performance for robustness

The simulator produces this trade in every pass: rv04_mid is the best nominal design and the worst deployable one; the retention screen’s leaders lose to plainer hands under full error; sv1_u0100’s promotion was an artifact of the clip handicap. The bench has never tested the trade as a comparison. It is a two-hand sitting: the nominal leader and the robustness leader of one pass, ten trials each under deliberately perturbed staging (±3 mm along the tool, ±8° of yaw, the ensemble’s own distribution), scored on retention. If the robustness ranking predicts the bench and the nominal ranking does not, the search objective changes from nominal cosine to ensemble win, which is a one-line change in real_v1_select_budget.py.

The bi-level plan (Section III-B)

Kept as a plan. Jsim(m) exists for one task and one controller and is the funnel’s score; Jreal(m) exists for eight hands; Δ(m) is the eight signed differences in Table IV. The plan’s claim that a common optimisation budget keeps control quality from deciding the ranking is the lesson of July stated as a rule, and the derived controller satisfies it trivially. The two open decisions are which inner solver runs on the population (the derived plan is the only one that has run at population scale; RL has run on nine designs of this family) and which outer loop reads Δ(m), which is item (ii). Nothing in the plan has been executed beyond one iteration of its first step.

Three of these were built as the same thing at different times: the mkdocs site (April), the webpaper (June) and the engineering logs (June) each claimed to be the canonical readable account, and each stopped being maintained when the work moved. The one that survived every move is the dated result page, because it is written once, regenerates from its own data, and is never expected to stay current: a page from 2026-09-06 says what was known on 2026-09-06, and a retraction is an edit to that page plus a mark on its index row.

Proposal: three roles, one tree, one index

  1. Record (immutable, dated). docs/experiments/YYYYMMDD-topic/ stays the unit of record for every experiment, positive, negative and retracted, with the builder + template pattern and a row in INDEX.md. A folder may also hold a README.md that says how to re-run, never what was found. The July loose summaries get folded into dated pages (one page per sweep, built from the JSON that still exists) and the engineering logs are frozen under docs/archive/ with no new appends; new findings go to a page, and a page can be one table long.
  2. State (mutable, small). One docs/STATE.md per track (design search, control, hardware), at most one screen each, rewritten rather than appended: what is running, what is blocked on what, the three next measurements, and dated pointers into the record. This replaces RESEARCH_STATE.md, the session handoffs, the queue files and the STATUS logs. A state file older than the newest record page in its track is stale by definition, and the INDEX builder can print that.
  3. Reference (mutable, slow). docs/reference/ for what does not change with each experiment: the task definition, the plant and its calibration, the funnel, the bench protocol, the training pipeline, the registry of hands and policies, the gotcha list. These are the webpaper’s chapters and this page’s §2–§7, rebuilt as HTML from the same template so they publish like everything else. The Typst layer and the mkdocs layer retire into the archive; their durable content moves here once.

Four rules that make the three roles hold. Every published artifact is a file in the repo first and its URL sits on its INDEX row (already the rule). The session memory is mirrored into the repo as docs/reference/lessons/, one file per lesson with the same front matter, so the retractions and the “why” travel with the code and a collaborator can read them. The paper/ tex sources are tracked (a private branch or a submodule if the PDF must stay out), since the corrections that change the paper’s numbers currently exist only on one disk. And the INDEX becomes the single entry point for the repo: docs/INDEX.md lists the three reference pages, the three state files, and every record page newest first, with a linter that fails on a page without a local file, a URL that appears twice, or an undated folder. This page is the first reference page, and the funnel, definitions and pipeline sections of it are the ones that should be maintained rather than re-derived.
