A camera gives you angles. A tape measure gives you a frame.

An AprilTag reports its pose in the camera's frame — a frame that moves whenever anyone nudges the tripod. Four numbers, recorded by hand, turn that into something the simulator can be held to.

The load-bearing one is the fingertip datum, because it is the only measurement that ties the two floors together through a part both sides model. With the distal link vertical, its top flat edge sits 65.0 mm above the bench floor. In real_hand_morphology_actuated.xml at the zero pose that same edge is at 21.0 mm, and the 37.2 mm link runs down from there to 16.2 mm below the floor — which is the scene builder's own "straight fingers put every pad 16 mm below the floor" note, arrived at from the other direction.

So z_bench = z_sim + 44.0, the physical palm plane stands 106.5 mm over its table where the simulator's stands at 62.5 over its own, and the bench scenes' 100 mm post is 144 mm on the floor. One subtraction, written down in one place, and a height measured on the bench becomes a height the sim predicted.

That is the floor — a static target at 320 mm with no motion smear, not what a swinging shaft will give. But it settles the only question that mattered before spending a bench session: the differences simulation predicts between the shipped plans are 12° apart, and the instrument resolves them by three orders of magnitude.

Two things it will not tell you

What the sensor refuses

The tag is not the object

The cylinder tag rides a vane 21 mm past the flat end face — 71 mm from the centre along the shaft. Through a 90° turn the tag sweeps 71 mm while the cylinder's centre barely moves. Reporting the tag's position as the object's would put a phantom 70 mm translation in every trace, and it would look exactly like a slip.

Bench x and y are withheld

A single static tag supplies world up, and the tape supplies a height datum. It cannot supply its own heading about vertical — that is one unknown short of a full frame. So heights and radial distances are reported and (x, y) comes back as None, not as a plausible-looking zero, until --calibrate-heading solves it against a known staged position.

Why that second one is a design decision, not a limitation

A fabricated heading would print believable x/y numbers beside real z ones, in the same table, in the same units, with nothing distinguishing them. The geometry module returns None and the web app prints no heading calibrated in the slot where the coordinate would go.

So can the policy run on hardware?

Not yet — and the tags were never what stopped it

The observation problem is solved, and it turns out to have been the smallest of four.

  1. Rate

    The tags run at 30 Hz and the policy at 50. The servo bus sustains about 111 Hz of sync writes, but sync reads do not work on the SCS0009 at all, so joint positions cost individual reads.

  2. Plant

    The yaw joints arrive at 0.44–0.90 of what they are told — measured over 20 bench runs, and it is torque rather than speed. A policy trained where commanded equals achieved is driving a different hand, feedback or no feedback.

  3. The policy itself

    b33 was shown to ignore its observations: replaying the whole 66-dimensional input from a different rollout cost nothing. Handing a policy that ignores its inputs a real sensor changes nothing about what it does.

  4. Safety

    Nothing gates a streamed policy against finger-to-finger collision, and three of the four originally deployed designs interpenetrate along their own planned path.

The tags make a sighted policy trainable; they do not make an open-loop one closed. What they open is not "stream b33 at 50 Hz" — it is that the object-derived columns are now a training signal that survives deployment, so a policy trained to use them is no longer trained on information the bench cannot supply.

Separately, and by a different mechanism

A bound nobody chose was deciding how far the hand may turn

Two plans shipped on 30 August would not load. HandRuntime.load_plan raises on any validation failure, and both overran the aa/yaw command range — by 3.88° and 6.27°, both at turn_end, both on middle yaw.

That range was not a hardware limit. It was ±70°, a conservative cap set before any plan had been driven; the declared contract — the one the MJCF still encodes and a test pins — is ±85°. And the manual torque-free sweep had put servo 6, the one both plans need, through −162.6° to +136.8° by hand. So the cap was not protecting a servo. It was a screening variable, exactly like the ±0.5 rad residual clip one layer up.

The gap in the evidence, stated rather than papered over

Thumb aa was never swept past −70.02° — the manual sweep records where a person stopped moving a freed joint, not necessarily where the joint does. No deployed plan commands it past −23.1°, so nothing rides on it today. If one ever does, sweep servo 0 again before trusting the bound.

What a bench session does now

Two readings of the same run, and their disagreement

Tracking is on by default and needs no flags. The session finds an interpreter that can open the camera, arms the tracker before the motion so the first frames are the staged pose, and stops it by signal when the run ends. Each repeat leaves a per-frame CSV, a summary, and the tracker's own stdout in the session directory.

The operator prompt survives, and it now offers the measured angle as its default instead of asking for a recollection. Both readings are kept. The tracker cannot see a shaft that turned by slipping through the fingers rather than with them; it reports a dropout as missing data where the operator sees a dropped shaft; it has no opinion about whether two fingers touched. When the two differ by more than 10°, the session says so at the prompt and records the difference.

On the station, samples arriving during a run are appended to that run's own log as kind: "object", so the trace and the joint commands share one timeline, and capabilities.object_pose is derived from whether data is arriving rather than from a literal — the camera can be unplugged, aimed at a wall, or looking at a shadowed tag, and in each of those the station must not claim an object sensor.

And overnight, on a separate thread

The population doubled, and one new hand took the top

The Sobol sequence is prefix-stable, so extending 4,096 → 8,192 is a strict superset: the first half keeps its tags and its scenes, and only the new half costs anything. 6,629 hands inside the measured rails, 57,220 screened cells, 535 passing at some clip, 227 confirmed — roughly double the 269 → 119 of the first population, which is what a doubled sample should do and is worth stating precisely, because it means the search is not saturating.

sv1_u1939 came out of the first pass as the full-error leader at a 0.35 win rate, clip 0.70. It came out of this one at 0.35 at clip 0.70, from an independently launched run. The ranking is not decoration.

The non-transfer result, again

Six new leaders were exported at the clips the retention screen chose; five passed every deployment gate; and when they were band-scanned on the bench schedule — fixed palm, tool on a post, 9.6 s hold — two of five kept the tool at any clip at all. Yesterday the same figure was three of sixteen. The two maneuvers keep disagreeing about which hands work, and anything selected under one has to be re-scanned under the other before it is shipped.
