# The reorientation primitive — a hand-free description of what our reorienters do
2026-08-27.  Tool: `scripts/reorient_primitive.py` (extract | compare | execute | feasibility)
Data: `results/reorient_primitive/*.npz`, figures + primitives `docs/experiments/20260827-reorient_primitive/`

GOAL: an object-centric, morphology-agnostic representation of our best reorientation policies,
so that a design can be evaluated by RE-TARGETING a known skill instead of re-learning one
(currently ~5.4 GPU-h per design, seed-dominated).

Sources: b33 on m05 (driven through its live-A seam by a10, handoff step 58) and r4 on perp.
32 envs x 400 steps each, deterministic, each env rebuilt from its OWN config.yaml.


## 1. The representation

A cylinder is symmetric about its long axis, so an absolute azimuth in the object's body frame is
not observable — it is whatever yaw the shaft spawned with. The datum that IS observable, and is
the natural one for this task, is where UP is as seen by the shaft: project world +Z into the
shaft's cross-section and call that direction theta = 0. In that TILT FRAME each fingertip has

    theta   which side of the shaft it rides (0 = upper, +-180 = lower)
    s       how far along the shaft from the centre of mass
    f       its contact load

and the whole primitive is these three, per finger, indexed by PHASE (the shaft's alignment with
vertical) rather than by clock. Phase-indexing matters: two hands that trace the same path at
different speeds are the same primitive, and indexed by phase the record becomes a feedback law
— desired contact configuration as a function of object state — instead of an open-loop demo.
(The existing `src/morphohand/rl/imitation.py` reference is the clock-indexed version, which is
why it can only be a shaping reward.) The primitive also carries the object's pose in the PALM
frame per phase, which is what makes a kinematic feasibility test possible at all.

VALIDATED AS A DESCRIPTION. Rebuilding each fingertip's position from the primitive at the
recorded phase reproduces where the tip actually was to a median **3.0 mm (m05) / 2.0 mm (perp)**.
The description is faithful.


## 2. What the two reference policies actually do

                                   b33 on m05        r4 on perp
  peak / final held-cos            0.931 / 0.898     0.983 / 0.965
  envs that reorient AND hold      13/32             30/32
  loaded fingers                   2.79              1.96  (thumb 0.0 N throughout)
  total contact force              21 N              10 N
  roll / tilt (body-frame omega)   1.56              1.02
  WRIST share of the achieved tilt 0.0%              0.0%

- **The wrist does none of it.** Both policies carry `palm_rotation_residual_scale 0.3` and both
  use it for zero of the turn. A fingertip/object representation is therefore the right one —
  that had been an assumption, it is now measured.
- **m05 rolls, perp does not.** Roll/tilt 1.56 vs 1.02, and the schedules say the same thing:
  m05's three tips sweep ~45-50 deg azimuthally around the shaft at a near-fixed axial position;
  perp's azimuths are static to within ~10 deg and its two loaded contacts sit parked at
  s = +42 mm, sliding ~5 mm over the whole reorient.
- **perp's thumb reads exactly 0.0 N at every phase.** The opposed pair does the entire task.


## 3. The finding that reassigns credit

CONTROL, measured first: the scripted grasp + scripted lift, with no policy at all, reorients by
**cos 0.00** on both hands. Everything below is policy work.

Splitting each rollout at the step where the shaft's LOWEST POINT leaves the floor
(z_low = z_c - (L|cos| + R*sqrt(1-cos^2)), L=50 mm, R=12.5 mm — not the centre height, which is
still low when a 100 mm shaft is half upright):

                            gain while still touching the floor   gain once fully in hand
  b33 on m05                        +0.643  (69%)                       +0.288 (31%)
  r4 on perp                        +0.452  (46%)                       +0.532 (54%)

On m05 the floor phase is over in the first 4 control steps (cos 0 -> 0.61 at z = 0.025) and it is
**Policy A's** grasp closing that does it, not the reorienter. b33 contributes the last third.
The known v1 floor-bracing failure mode (`project_inhand_reorient_v1`) was never actually
designed out; it survives into the reference policy.

**Opposite gravitational regimes.** Angle between (object COM - force-weighted contact centroid)
and world -Z, over the airborne part:

  r4 on perp   70 -> 57 -> 41 -> 30 -> 21 deg   |COM-centroid| ~45 mm   -> the shaft HANGS,
               settling monotonically toward vertical from a grip near its end
  b33 on m05  121 -> 136 -> 162 -> 162 deg      |COM-centroid| ~20 mm   -> the COM is ABOVE the
               grip; the shaft is balanced inverted and must be actively rolled up

One object-centric number — the signed axial offset of the grip from the centre of mass — selects
the strategy, and explains the rest: a hanging end-grip has nothing resisting axial slip (perp's
documented slow slip, and its every-rollout drop with the thumb idle), while an inverted balance
needs three loaded fingers (m05's 21 N on 2.8 contacts).


## 4. Executing the primitive as a controller (`execute`)

Runs in CPU MuJoCo on any hand: read phase, read the schedule at phase + lookahead, build the tip
targets on the shaft's surface, damped-least-squares IK per finger, apply **as a residual on the
grasp anchor**. Nothing in the loop names a hand except the tip Jacobian and the pad radius.

  b33 primitive on m05     peak 0.582 / final 0.574, held        (b33 itself: 0.931 / 0.898)
  r4  primitive on perp    peak 0.322 / final 0.220, held 11 N   (r4 itself:  0.983 / 0.965)

**Faithful description, incomplete generator: ~60% of m05's turn and ~30% of perp's.** Adding
the force half of the schedule (impedance: force error -> radial target offset) helps perp
(0.22 -> 0.32) and hurts m05. What the schedule cannot encode is exactly what section 3 found:
the floor interaction during the grasp, and an actively managed pivot under gravity.

TWO TRAPS, both of which produced convincing wrong answers first:
- **Never replace the grasp anchor with the IK solution.** These are position servos; the grip
  force IS the commanded-minus-actual joint error that the squeezing anchor supplies. Commanding
  the tip exactly onto the surface sets that error to ~0 and the hand lets go. The first version
  did this and dropped the shaft in every single run, with IK residuals of 300-1500 mm that
  looked like "this hand cannot reach" but were entirely post-drop.
- **peak_cos is not a result without the height and the force.** A dropped shaft lands upright
  and reads cos +1.000. Several sweep cells here peaked at 0.96-1.00 with final_z 0.0124 and
  0.00 N. Only report peak alongside final_z and contact count.
- Also: a phase grid wider than the policy's own coverage leaves NaN at the table ends, and
  np.interp then poisons EVERY query. Primitives are trimmed to their populated span.

Grip placement alone is not a substitute either: a scripted grasp swept over axial offset x grip
tightness (12 cells, no policy) tops out at held cos **0.249**. r4 is actively managing the
pivot, not merely permitting it.


## 5. Kinematic feasibility as a design metric — NEGATIVE

`feasibility` asks, with no physics and no policy, whether a hand can put its loaded fingertips on
the scheduled surface points while the shaft sits at the scheduled palm-relative pose. Seconds per
design, no seed. This is the task-CONDITIONED repair of the UHAS authority metric, which was
task-blind and correlated -0.03 with goal approach.

  design    trained held-cos       mean residual   p90     met(<5mm)   limit saturation
  m05       0.891                     1.05 mm     2.55 mm    91.7%          6.1%
  H06_04    0.748                     0.22 mm     0.70 mm   100.0%          2.2%
  L01_13    0.720                     0.39 mm     0.10 mm    96.1%          2.6%
  G02_00    0.482                     2.22 mm     8.73 mm    84.4%          8.5%
  sp25      0.48 (n=1)                0.80 mm     3.42 mm    97.8%          0.7%
  H06_08    phantom 1-finger          1.49 mm     6.86 mm    87.8%          5.2%
  H06_06    lift-hostile              1.07 mm     5.16 mm    89.4%          3.9%

**Every design reaches the schedule to within 1-2 mm on a 12.5 mm-radius shaft, and the reference
design m05 scores mid-pack.** Reachability is saturated; it does not discriminate. This converges
with the link-length geometry gate of the same day (`8-27_LINK_LENGTH_GATE.txt`): once the hand is
modelled properly, geometry stops ranking designs and only dynamics separate them. Do not build a
design search on this score.


## 6. Where this leaves the universalisation question

The hand-agnostic content of these two policies is smaller than the premise assumed. What
transfers is the schedule as a DESCRIPTION — which is a good evaluator and a good prior, and is
exactly why the existing clock-indexed imitation reward worked (tightest seed bands, +-0.02).
What does not transfer is the generative part, because 31-54% of the measured turn is produced by
interactions the fingertips do not author: the floor during the grasp, and gravity on an
off-centre grip.

There is no single primitive across the two topologies, and the reason is not that the
representation is too weak. It is that the two policies are solving different mechanical problems
— one balances the shaft inverted and rolls it up, the other hangs it and manages the settle.

Consequences worth acting on:
1. **Any reorient number in this program is at most half a finger-driven reorientation.** If
   in-hand reorientation is the actual goal, the floor phase has to be excluded by construction
   (a no-floor-contact assertion, or a lift-to-clearance before the reorient reward opens), which
   `project_inhand_reorient_v1` recommended in 2026 and was never implemented.
2. **Scoring finger lengths on "can it execute a finger-driven rotation" scores 31-54% of the
   behaviour.** The larger determinants are where along the shaft the hand can grip and whether it
   can manage the grip through the floor-assisted flip.
3. The signed grip offset from the object's COM, in units of the object's half-length, is the one
   object-centric scalar found here that both transfers and predicts the strategy. It is untested
   as a design variable.
