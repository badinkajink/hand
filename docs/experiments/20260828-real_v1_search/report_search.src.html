Which hands turn the shaft

108 hands sampled over the CAD gantry workspace, each given a grasp sweep and a schedule sweep, each rollout repeated under spawn jitter. 49 of them stand the shaft up and keep it. The thing that decides it is not the mount geometry — it is where the thumb pad sits along the shaft.

Companion to Two Routes to Vertical (methods) and MECHANISM.md. Section 1 corrects three measurements in those; every number below is re-measured under the protocol in section 4.

01 — supersedes

Three ways the earlier numbers were measured wrong

All three were found by trying to reproduce the 2026-08-28 open-loop table and failing. None of them is a code bug; all three are protocol.

1.1 The controller in the published rows is the linear anchor, not the IK carry

Every row in carry_pospole.json and carry_others_pospole.json — the rows that established "the raised pivot works" — carries max_ik_residual_mm: 0.0 and commanded_tip_travel_mm: {0,0,0}. Those fields are only ever zero in the --linear-anchor branch, which skips the per-step IK entirely and lerps the finger joints in a straight line between two set-points. So the schedule that produced the good numbers is the one LerpFingerActionCfg.hold_target_ctrl already implements, and the reorient needs no runtime IK at all.

This matters beyond bookkeeping, because the two controllers do not merely differ in quality — they differ in whether the hand keeps hold of the object. Same design, same cell, same grasp; the only difference is how the fingers are commanded between the two ends:

{{GRIP_DECAY_ROWS}}
rv05_manual, CEM grip, pivot height 8.2 mm, −90°, held and otherwise undisturbed. Tip force is the sum of the three pad normal forces. grip_decay_rv05.json.
hold IK cosIK zIK padsIK force lin coslin zlin padslin force

Why the IK carry lets go. The probe's own rule is that the IK solution must be applied as a residual on the grasp anchor, because these are position servos and the grip force is the commanded-minus-actual error. Re-solving the IK every step re-centres the command on where the pad actually is, so the error — and the force with it — decays to nothing: 16.1 → 10.9 → 6.0 → 1.3 → 0 N, and the shaft is on the table at 1.2 s. The linear anchor commands a fixed end pose that stays inside the object, so it keeps pressing.

The second thing the table shows is that the turn is not finished when the command finishes. On the linear anchor the shaft goes on settling into vertical against a still-loaded grip — 0.837 at the end of the sweep, 0.999 half a second later. A schedule scored at the end of its own sweep is scored early. Across the 58 hands that turn the shaft as a pinch-roll, a mean of 17% of the final alignment arrives after the command stops.

1.2 A 400-step hold cannot see the release

The old ok test asked for ≥2 pad contacts and a final height, both read at the last step of a 0.8 s hold. Two failures hide behind that. A shaft that comes upright and then slides out of the grip over the next second reads fine (the IK column above is exactly this case). And a shaft carried on the middle phalanges with the pads off it — which is the normal end state of a raised-pivot turn — reads as a drop. ok is now the object's minimum height over a 1.6 s hold, with contact counted against the whole hand.

1.3 One rollout is not a measurement

The good cells sit in narrow resonances of (pivot height, turn angle). On rv05_manual at −90°, neighbouring pivot heights read 0.991 and 0.000. A single rollout therefore reports whichever side of a resonance it landed on, and best-of-a-grid reports the luckiest draw. --repeats perturbs the object's spawn by σ = 0.5 mm and reports mean, sd and how many draws kept the shaft.

1.4 The five studied hands, re-measured

Linear anchor, 1.6 s hold, n = 8 jittered draws, best cell per design over 8 pivot heights × 3 turn angles. The ordering is unchanged from the single-draw table and the separation is now resolved: sd is 0.002–0.021 on every row but one, an order of magnitude below the gaps. The exception is rv00_wide at the narrow straddle, whose best cell is bimodal — it turns the shaft fully or loses it, and the mean of 0.454 describes neither draw.

02 — mechanism

You lose the descending pad, and that is fine

The bound in MECHANISM.md is right and its consequence was read wrong. Rotate the shaft by θ about the pinch axis with the pads on fixed material points, and the pair contact on the descending side moves down by straddle · sin θ, which its finger can only follow by extending. The mount-to-pad chain is 68.11 mm straight and these grasps sit 1–7 mm short of it, so the descending pad runs out after a few degrees.

Under the commanded −90° turn the descending pad is the one at +y, which the fitter calls index: a contact at (13, +40, 0) mm maps to (13, 0, −40) mm. What actually happens is not that the turn stops at the ceiling. The index pad simply leaves, and the turn continues on the other two. Measured across the 49 hands that reorient:

{{TOUCH_ROWS}}

groupnthumb onindex on middle onthumb driveindex drivemiddle drive

Every hand that reorients does it as a two-point couple between the thumb and the ascending pad. The index pad — the descending one, the one the extension budget is about — is on the shaft for 19–28% of the turn and carries 6–9% of the tangential force in all 49 of them. It is a spectator. The extension bound does not predict how far the shaft turns; it predicts which contact you are going to lose.

The discriminator between a hand that turns the shaft and one that does not is contact persistence, not friction. Reorienting hands keep the thumb and the ascending pad on the shaft 92% of the turn; the ones that fail keep them on 48–49% and drop the shaft 68 mm. Friction saturation is nearly irrelevant at this scale: only 7 of 80 designs push any pad to ≥0.98 of its friction cone, and 6 of those 7 reorient anyway.

That last point is a correction to the reading of the middle-finger slip observed in the trained policies. The saturation was real — it was measured at 0.74 and 1.00 on the pads that had to supply the couple — but across a population it is a symptom of the grip being wrong, not the binding constraint on its own.

03 — the knob

Slide the thumb 20 mm along the shaft

A pad at the shaft's mid-length has moment arm |ryz| = 0 about the pinch axis. It can press as hard as it likes and contribute nothing to standing the shaft up. That is exactly where the fitter put the thumb: tip_targets placed all three pads on one ring, thumb from −x, pair straddling it at ±spread. So the hand's largest normal force had no arm, and the two pads that did have an arm were the ones with the least force.

--thumb-axial slides only the thumb along the shaft. On rv04_mid — same design, same straddle, same pivot height, same turn — it is the difference between no reorient and a solved one:

{{THUMB_ARM_ROWS}}
Contact traces of the two rollouts above, one draw each. traces/rv04_mid_thAx{0,20}.json.
quantitythumb @0thumb @20 reading

The offset does not primarily add thumb torque. It redistributes normal force onto the pad that has to supply the couple: the middle pad's mean normal force doubles, its peak friction-cone utilisation falls from 1.00 (sliding) to 0.70, and it stays on the shaft for 92% of the turn instead of 45%. This is the fix that was proposed from the trained-policy slip trace — move force to the pair rather than raise total grip — obtained by geometry instead of by control.

It is not a free win, and the population says so. Over 3564 cells matched on (design, straddle, depth, pivot height, angle), the offset is worse on average: held cos −0.023 (Wilcoxon p = 1.4 × 10⁻⁹), shaft kept in 25.3% of cells against 35.9%. It narrows the band of schedules that work and raises what the best of them reaches. At the design level — best cell of each arm, 74 designs with both — it gains +0.078, which does not clear significance (p = 0.11), but 32 of the 49 hands that reorient at all use it, and it is on all 15 of the top 15 cells.

05 — scoring

What predicts a design, and what does not

Every candidate score is computed from the settled grasp alone — one 650-step rollout, no schedule, no policy, no seed — and is then compared against the carry it claims to stand in for. Spearman ρ is against the best cell's held cos over the 80 graspable designs; AUC is P(a reorienting design scores above a non-reorienting one), so 0.5 carries no information.

{{SCORE_ROWS}}
n = 80 designs. REPORT.md §"What scores a design".
scoredefinitionρp AUC

The thumb's moment arm about the pinch axis is the score. ρ = +0.443 (p = 3.9 × 10⁻⁵), AUC 0.821 — it separates a reorienting hand from a non-reorienting one four times out of five, from one forward-kinematics call and one contact query on a static grasp.

tau_thumb = mu * f_n,thumb * |r_yz,thumb|          [N mm]

  mu            pad friction coefficient (2.4 in this scene)
  f_n,thumb     thumb pad normal force at the settled, lifted grasp
  |r_yz,thumb|  distance from the shaft's centre to the thumb contact,
                projected out of the pinch axis  ->  the moment arm

the same quantity for the pair is tau_pair; their sum is the friction-limited
couple the grasp can apply about the axis the shaft has to rotate around.

Three things about it are worth stating plainly.

  • It is a property of the grasp, not of the hand. That is the right unit — the whole result of this study is that the grasp decides the turn — but it means it cannot rank mount layouts on their own. It ranks (design × grasp) pairs, which is what a design search on this hand should be enumerating anyway.
  • The thumb term beats the pair term (AUC 0.821 against 0.555) and beats their sum (0.604). The pair always has an arm; whether the thumb has one is the variable.
  • The previously published metric does not generalise. ceiling = asin(extension_left / straddle) reported 9/9 agreement on nine cells of one three-knob family. At n = 80 it reads ρ = +0.096, AUC 0.617 — better than chance and much weaker than advertised. The 9/9 held because that grid varied X separation, which happens to move both the extension budget and the grasp; it was not a validation.
06 — styles

They do not all turn it the same way

An earlier attempt labelled the styles by the pads' carry fraction — 1 − (pad travel across the shaft's surface) / (surface arc the turn sweeps), which separates a true carry, contacts fixed on the material, from a spin under stationary pads. It does not work on this hand: the pads come on and off the shaft and walk along it as well as around it, so 72 of 80 designs land in one bucket at about −1, meaning "travelled roughly twice the arc". What does separate them, and what the renders show, is how many pads are on the shaft and where the shaft goes in the grip.

{{STYLE_ROWS}}
n = 80 graspable designs, best cell each. "Shaft dz" is the object's height change over the commanded turn; "settles" is the fraction of the final alignment that arrives after the command stops.
stylenreorientheld cos pads on shaft T/I/Mcontactsshaft dz what it is

The taxonomy collapses to one working style and three failures. Every hand that reorients is a pinch-roll: two pads loaded, the descending one shed early, the shaft rolling between the thumb and the ascending pad while the whole grip rotates. Single is the ordinary failure — the grip does not survive the first few degrees and the shaft falls 68 mm. Palm-pin is the interesting one: the shaft rides up 15–19 mm and levers itself against the palm plate, which pins it and stops the turn. Those hands hold perfectly and reorient not at all.

07 — confirmation

Does it survive a CEM grasp

The search's grip comes from the geometric fitter, which is a floor: on the earlier landscape the same cell read 0.575 fitted and 0.909 with its CEM grip. So the two most interesting designs were re-run end to end — fit the thumb-offset pose, CEM the grasp on it (120 iterations, population 80), then sweep 8 pivot heights × 3 angles at n = 8 — on copies of the scenes, because the fitter writes open_ik into the file it is given and the swept scenes must not move under the sweep.

{{CEM_ROWS}}
cem_cells_*.json. "one cell" is the cell the fitted grip preferred; "swept" is the best of 24 on the CEM grip.
designfitted gripCEM, one cell CEM, sweptsdkeptread

ax_asym−10 confirms at 0.936 ± 0.074 on a real optimised grasp, which puts a design nobody had sampled between rv05_manual (0.991) and rv03_narrowy (0.951). Its geometry is the CAD-nominal hand with the index and middle mounts slid 10 mm together along the shaft — an asymmetric pair, which the three-knob compact family cannot express and which nothing in the study had ever tried.

rv04_mid does not. Its fitted-grip 0.997 falls to 0.625 once CEM has optimised the grasp. CEM's objective is hold quality, and on this hand hold quality and turn quality trade against each other — the same conflict fit_real_v1_pose already had, where the straddle was chosen by scoring resistance to "the shaft levering about the pinch axis", which is the reorientation. The first single-cell attempt read 0.008 and would have been reported as a refutation; it was a resonance-selection artifact, and sweeping the cells is not optional.

Open, and it is the next thing to fix. A grasp optimiser that scores only the hold will keep giving back rotational locks. The obvious repair is to add tau_thumb, or the open-loop carry itself, to the CEM objective — it costs one 1600-step rollout per candidate, which is the same order as the hold probe CEM already runs.

08 — reading

What this changes for the build and for the policy

Hardware

  • Keep thumb-to-pair X separation above about 55 mm. At 40 mm no grasp has any extension budget and none of the six hands turns anything. Above that the gantry layout stops mattering — the 49 working hands span X 55–120 mm.
  • The thumb gantry's Y travel is load-bearing and was never used. Every design in the study to date sat at thumb y = 0, the one place where the thumb cannot torque the shaft. The CAD workspace gives it ±55 mm.
  • Plan for a two-point turn. The third pad is off the shaft for three quarters of every successful reorient. Pad compliance and friction on the thumb and the ascending finger are what matter; the descending finger is along for the ride.
  • The reorient wants a lower palm than the grasp does. Grip depth trades clearance for extension budget, and the fitter spends all of it on clearance.

Policy

  • No runtime IK. The working schedule is a straight line in joint space between two set-points, which is LerpFingerActionCfg.hold_target_ctrl with hold_switch_from_sim_step. The per-step IK carry is both worse and unstable.
  • Over-rotation is a free knob. 22 of the 32 best cells with a thumb offset command −100° rather than −90°: driving past vertical walks the pads back down the far side of the shaft instead of parking them at the top, where the grip self-extinguishes.
  • Score checkpoints on a held minimum, over ≥1.6 s. Both the release and the settle live in the second after the command ends.
  • What RL is for here is regulation. The open loop reaches 0.99 in a resonance two millimetres of pivot height wide, from a scripted delivery. Absorbing that is a residual policy's job, and it is why the trained anchored policies have sd 0.003–0.031 where the schedule alone has 0.46.

Not done

  • No policy was trained on any of this. The 49 hands are open-loop results from a scripted delivery. Whether the thumb offset survives Policy A's real delivery — which is where the schedule previously flipped poles on rv05_manual — is untested.
  • The object is still 24.5 g. Doubling its mass drops the reference carry, and a real screwdriver is heavier. This is the one axis known to fail and it invalidates nothing else until it is fixed.
  • n = 3 in the sweep, n = 8 on the confirmations. The sweep ranks; it does not measure. Any design promoted from it needs the n = 8 pass, and the two that got it moved (0.997 → 0.625 on one, holding on the other).
  • The style taxonomy is 58 / 19 / 2 / 1. Three of the four buckets are too small to say anything about, and the interesting one — palm-pin, the hands that hold perfectly and refuse to turn — has two members.
