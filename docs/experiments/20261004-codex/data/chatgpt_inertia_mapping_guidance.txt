Yes. The new result clarifies that what you have done is not merely a better approximation of what diagexact does. You are solving a slightly different problem.
diagexact asks:
“Given the user-authored MuJoCo impedance \(d\), what is the correct current constraint-space inertia to use so that this dimensionless acceleration-level impedance is realized accurately?”
Your compensation asks:
“Given a desired physical spring stiffness \(k_i\) in N/m from the Winkler quadrature, what MuJoCo impedance \(d_i\) should I use at this particular current constraint inertia so that the resulting static force law is \(F_i=k_i\delta_i\)?”
Those are not the same objective.
For your static contact model, the measured relationship is essentially
\[ k_{\rm actual,i} = \frac{1} {t_c^2(1-d_i)\,A_{ii}}, \]
where
\[ A_{ii} = J_iM^{-1}J_i^T \]
is the current diagonal of the Delassus / constraint-space inverse inertia. Your earlier experiments verified this scaling essentially exactly.
So if you simply enable diagexact, MuJoCo replaces its approximate \(A_{ii}\) with the correct runtime \(A_{ii}\) when constructing the constraint regularization. That is exactly what the documentation says: it replaces the compile-time body-based approximation with the exact diagonal of \(A=JM^{-1}J^T\) at the current configuration. [MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/3.13.0/changelog.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
But you have still authored a fixed \(d_i\).
Therefore,
\[ A_{ii}\uparrow \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_{\rm actual}\downarrow \]
and vice versa. diagexact makes the inertia number more correct; it does not cancel the fact that MuJoCo's soft-constraint force scale depends on that inertia.
You start one layer higher, from the physical model:
\[ p = K\delta, \qquad k_i=K A_i^{\rm surface}. \]
For your spherical Drake-matching foundation,
\[ k_i \approx \frac{E}{R}A_i^{\rm surface}. \]
That gives a stiffness in actual physical units, N/m.
Then, instead of treating solimp as fixed, you solve the MuJoCo equation backwards for the impedance that realizes that physical stiffness:
\[ \boxed{ d_i(q) = 1- \frac{1} {t_c^2\,k_i\,A_{ii}(q)} } \]
(modulo the exact conventions/corrections in your implementation).
Now substitute it back:
\[ k_{\rm actual,i} = \frac{1} {t_c^2 \left( \frac{1}{t_c^2 k_i A_{ii}} \right) A_{ii}} = k_i. \]
The inertia cancels.
That's why you get 817.21 N/m from 0.25× through 4× mass rather than merely a more accurately inertia-scaled MuJoCo constraint.
So I would describe what you've built as something like:
physical-stiffness normalization of native MuJoCo soft contacts
or, more specifically,
Delassus-conditioned contact impedance mapping
rather than saying you have “better diagexact.”
diagexact gives you the thing you need:
\[ A_{ii}(q). \]
Your contribution is using it to implement the mapping
\[ \boxed{ (k_i,t_c,A_{ii}(q)) \rightarrow d_i(q) } \]
so that a fixed physical constitutive law survives changing object inertia and robot configuration.
And there is a nice conceptual distinction here that I got wrong earlier.
MuJoCo's intended parameterization is deliberately dynamics-normalized. Its impedance \(d\) controls interpolation between unconstrained and reference accelerations. The docs explicitly define
\[ R_{ii}=\frac{1-d_i}{d_i}\hat A_{ii}, \]
and say the purpose of the inertia term is to get the scaling of that acceleration-level constraint model right. [MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
You instead want a material-normalized representation:
\[ \delta_i \rightarrow F_i \]
should have the same stiffness regardless of whether the thing touching the fingertip is a 25 g screwdriver or a 100 g object.
So:
\[ \begin{array}{lll} \textbf{MuJoCo / diagexact:} & d\ \text{fixed} & A_{ii}(q)\rightarrow R_{ii} \\[4pt] \textbf{your mapping:} & k_{\rm physical}\ \text{fixed} & A_{ii}(q)\rightarrow d_i(q)\rightarrow R_{ii} \end{array} \]
That's a meaningful difference.
For representing a physical compliant material, apparently yes, in the particular static sense you've tested.
For MuJoCo's general purpose, no. diagexact is doing what it is supposed to do: accurately realize MuJoCo's dimensionless soft-constraint semantics. You are imposing an additional physical interpretation that MuJoCo itself does not promise.
I'd phrase it as:
diagexact fixes the estimate of constraint inertia; it does not by itself make solref/solimp correspond to an invariant physical N/m stiffness. We use the runtime Delassus diagonal to translate a desired area-weighted foundation stiffness into the appropriate MuJoCo impedance, which cancels that inertia dependence at static equilibrium.
That is quite clean scientifically.
And it actually makes the 3.9 result more interesting, not less. If diagexact had magically solved everything, then most of your mass-normalization work would have reduced to “turn on a new MuJoCo flag.” Instead, the new flag appears to provide the accurate runtime quantity that your physical mapping needs.
The architecture may therefore be:
\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{CAD surface quadrature}\\ \downarrow\\ k_i=(E/R)A_i\\ \downarrow\\ \text{runtime }A_{ii}=J_iM^{-1}J_i^T\\ \downarrow\\ \text{physical-to-MuJoCo impedance translation}\\ \downarrow\\ \text{native MuJoCo contact solve} \end{array}} \]
That's substantially more satisfying than “tuned 205 soft spheres until they looked like Drake.”
And Codex is now testing exactly the right place where this construction could stop working: once \(\ddot q\neq0\) and tangential forces hit the friction cone, the off-diagonal Delassus coupling and cone constraints enter. Static \(A_{ii}\)-normalization cannot simply cancel all of that.
If the upcoming brake/sliding results remain close to Drake without introducing a custom force solver, then I think this becomes genuinely compelling.
