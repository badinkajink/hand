Gates on a Deployable Residual

A standing account of what now blocks a sim2real residual reorientation policy, written for a reader with no hardware time. The short answer is that the question splits three ways, and only the last is blocked: a residual on one hand is mostly a base-schedule swap; a residual that controls many hands needs conditioning, not correct rankings; and only choosing a hand is gated, on bench measurements rather than compute or search algorithms.

The question splits in two, and only one half is blocked

"a residual RL policy for reorientation across a large set of hands"

These are separate claims with separate evidence and separate blockers, and conflating them is what makes the program look more stuck than it is.

Claim A is not gated on hardware. The actuator model is measured, corrected and validated against the bench on the one hand we have most data for. What remains is software that can be written today.

Claim B is not gated either, which took a second pass to see. A design-conditioned policy has to control each hand; it never has to say which hand is better. Conditioning is not ranking. The argument is developed in the value-gradient section below, and it is the single largest correction on this page.

Claim C is gated, and the gate is not "we need a better search algorithm." It is that the simulator does not currently order hands the way the bench orders them, and the measurement we would need to fix that — and, more importantly, to verify any fix — only exists on the bench.

What the plant work settled

2026-09-02, 236 archived runs — full detail in the sysid page

The servo is a spring with a cliff. Deflection is 0.0186° per unit of load, one constant for all nine joints, R² 0.94–0.99, no meaningful intercept. The mechanism is in the register file: p=15, i=0, d=15 — with no integral term a standing load leaves a proportional error forever. The cliff is protective_torque, which fires on 42% of middle_yaw bench runs.

Correcting the simulator means finger gain kp 30 → 0.5 (about 60× softer than shipped), forcerange 0.35, frictionloss 0.0035, and real link masses. On rv05 that closes the gap on both axes at once, where the shipped plant closed neither:

The cross-validation is the part worth trusting: kp was fitted to joint angles alone, and it independently lands where the whole-task outcome matches. Compliance turns out to be the carry mechanism, which is why the stiff shipped plant under-turns.

The limit of that result

It is a result about one hand's dynamics. It says the simulator can be made to reproduce rv05. It does not say the simulator can be made to compare hands, and the next section is the direct test of that.

The correction lifts the level. It does not fix the ordering.

seven designs, six trials each, replaying the exported per-step trajectory

Spearman against the bench moves −0.536 → +0.107 (n=7); over the five designs that run in both plants it is −0.300. Three designs land within about 0.1 of the bench. Four do not.

Both worst misses are slip cases, and they fail in opposite directions. sv1_u1364 is the design whose bench trials are not a distribution but a mixture — 0.21, 0.30, 0.32, 0.33, 0.61, 0.66, 0.70, 0.78, sd 0.221, roughly four times any other design's, from trials that held their grip and trials that slipped partway. The corrected simulator gives it 0.920 and retains every trial: it does not reproduce the slip at all. sv1_w0099 is the mirror — one of only two designs that dropped on the bench, and the corrected plant now drops it on every trial instead of some.

Do not compare this to +0.36

The transfer first pass reported spearman +0.36 (p = 0.43) on these same seven designs. That number came from a different harness — real_v1_deploy_envelope's rebuild with its own retention rule. This harness replays the exported trajectory, and its shipped baseline is −0.536, not +0.36. Only shipped-vs-corrected within one harness is a valid comparison. Both harnesses agree on the thing that matters: no usable relationship.

Two independent routes point at contact

and neither can be followed without the bench

The ranking failure is one route. The hysteresis measurement is the other, and it arrives at the same place from the opposite side.

A free-hanging finger's staircase shows a friction loop of 0.70–1.50°. Under a grasp, walking the plan's own chord forward and back, middle_yaw's loop is +12.26° mean, +14.06° max — about eleven times wider, while the load range grew only about fivefold. That excess cannot be attributed to the gearbox with the data in hand, because middle_pip shows an 8.16° loop at a load of only 109, which does not fit a friction model. The obvious remaining explanation is the object moving.

So the two routes agree: what the simulator is missing is contact, not actuator. And identifying contact means measuring slip, which means the hand.

Consequence, recorded deliberately

No further design search should be run against this simulator expecting it to rank hands. Neither plant ranks them. A faster or better-guided search over an objective with no demonstrated relationship to the bench converges more efficiently on a hand we cannot trust.

The hard gate is n = 7

the ceiling on every sim-fidelity claim this program can currently make

The entire bench validation set is 77 trials over 8 morphologies — 52 held, 6 dropped, 19 unresolved — of which 7 designs have a usable cos_hold. One of those seven (sv1_u1364) is a mixture rather than a distribution, and one more (g12) is 10/10 unresolved and dropped entirely.

This caps what can be concluded regardless of how much simulation is run. A spearman on n=7 has a 95% confidence interval spanning most of the achievable range; the transfer first pass's own p-value was 0.43. Any improvement to the contact model would have to be validated against seven numbers, and seven numbers cannot distinguish a real improvement from a reshuffle.

This is the sense in which the program is genuinely gated on physical testing, and it is worth stating plainly: more bench designs is the limiting resource for sim2real fidelity work, and no amount of GPU time substitutes for it.

What is not gated: the residual is a base-schedule swap

the machinery already exists; it is pointed at the wrong base

This was worth checking rather than assuming, and the finding is favourable. The action term in src/morphohand/rl/actions.py is already a bounded finger residual on top of a scripted set-point schedule, with a residual_active_from_sim_step gate for exactly the "let the script run, then let the policy correct" pattern. Its own docstring describes the reorient as a residual problem.

What it lacks is that its base schedule is a training-time scripted close, not the exported deployment plan. The three concrete pieces of work:

  • Reset from meta.replay_base_ctrl rather than the scene keyframe. This is already known to be load-bearing — the plant gate found the keyframe puts palm_pz at 0.01 against the plan's 0.1035, so a harness using the keyframe never lifts at all.
  • Drive the base from the plan's per-step _traj.csv, not the three-set-point chord. These are different paths — the clearance gate passes them separately, and a first ranking attempt was voided for replaying the chord.
  • Train on the corrected plant, with the residual clip stated as a maneuver-specific choice rather than inherited. The 0.5 rad figure was Policy B's action budget and has been hardcoded through every screen since; it helps the bench hold and hurts the proof-lift retention gate.

None of that needs the hand. It produces a residual for rv05 (or any hand with an exported plan), trained against a plant that demonstrably matches that hand on retention and alignment. That is a defensible deliverable on its own, and it is the cheapest real progress available right now.

What it would not be is a claim about transfer across designs. Stated as "a residual trained on a calibrated plant for a specific hand," it is honest and useful. Stated as "a residual that generalizes across morphologies," it is not supported.

What the deploy path can carry today

read before designing the policy's observation vector

The streaming endpoints exist (/api/v1/stream/{start,frame,end}), and the runtime announces the stream as "experimental write-only policy stream armed." That phrasing is exact. In the telemetry loop:

include_servos = idle or (not self._servo_writing.is_set()
                          and self._operation != "policy stream")

During a policy stream the runtime never reads the servos. A residual deployed on this path today gets no joint feedback. That is a deliberate half-duplex bus decision, not an oversight.

The important corollary is that this does not block a closed loop, because the object-pose sensor is not on that bus. The AprilTag tracker runs on the workstation and pushes already-reduced geometry to the station over the network, at 0.017° / 0.03 mm rms. So:

ObservationHardware sourceAvailable during a stream?
object pose / orientationAprilTags, over the networkyes — off the servo bus
joint positionsservo telemetry, half-duplex busno — suppressed by design
joint load / temperatureservo telemetryno — same bus
commanded posethe policy's own outputyes — trivially

An object-only residual is deployable on the path that already exists. This suits the evidence: b33 ignores its observations, and replaying the whole 66-dim input costs nothing. It also suits the failure mode the residual is meant to catch — the six bench drops all turn the shaft to a mean peak of +0.608, which is 37° of the 90° turn already won, and then collapse to +0.109. The residual does not need to discover the turn. It needs to notice the shaft escaping, which is precisely what the tags see and the open-loop plan is blind to.

Joint feedback in the loop is a separate, genuinely hardware-gated question: whether reads and writes can interleave on the half-duplex bus at 50 Hz. Sync writes are known not to be rate-limiting and reads have been clocked at 111 Hz, but interleaved at rate is unmeasured.

On sampling and ranking hands: the objective, not the algorithm

responding to the gradient-guided search question

The instinct is right that scoring-then-sorting is a weak way to search, and the literature in relevant_papers/ supports the direction. But the ordering of the fixes matters, and right now the search method is not the binding constraint.

A gradient tells you which way to move on the objective you have. Our objective is a simulator whose rank correlation with the bench is +0.107. Backpropagating through it moves faster along a direction we have not shown points anywhere. That is the ranking result's direct consequence, and it applies to any search method, gradient or otherwise.

The affordable version we have not done

Mannam's actual lever is not the genetic algorithm, it is policy transfer — and we have independently measured why that works. From-scratch reorient training has a between-draw sd of 0.3–0.5, which is why single-draw design rankings were noise and why the program costed a design search at n=48 replicates. Warmstarted, the between-draw sd is 0.032 — smaller than the 0.080 rollout noise. About two seeds then resolve a Δ of 0.1.

That makes genuine policy-in-the-loop ranking affordable today, with no gradients and no new simulator. It is roughly a 20× reduction in the cost of evaluating a design properly, and it is the single biggest unexploited lever in the search program.

The caveat is the same one as everywhere else on this page, and it is why this is listed as a lever rather than a plan: it makes the sim ranking cheap, not correct. Policy-in-the-loop evaluation resolves differences between designs faithfully in simulation. Whether those differences survive to the bench is the n=7 question.

The order these want to be done in

1. Make the evaluation trustworthy per design (warmstarted policy-in-the-loop — cheap, sim-only, available now). 2. Establish that sim differences predict bench differences (needs bench designs). 3. Then make the search efficient (gradients, GA, transfer). Doing 3 before 2 is the failure this page exists to prevent.

Value gradients, and the distinction that changes the answer

Bohlinger & Peters, Shape Your Body, arXiv:2606.00702

VGDS trains one embodiment-aware policy and value function across a robot distribution with ordinary PPO, then freezes the critic and uses it as a differentiable surrogate for the design. New designs are found by Adam ascent on the critic's prediction inside a soft trust region. 50 robots, design spaces of 190–1177 continuous parameters, 1–2 minutes per design instead of a retrain.

The important structural property: the gradient is through the critic network, not through contact. That sidesteps the blocker recorded against DiffMJX entirely — no differentiable simulator, no MJX port, nothing that MuJoCo-Warp cannot already do. It is the version of "backpropagate to guide the search" that is actually reachable from where this program stands.

The distinction I got wrong earlier on this page

Reading VGDS forces a split that the rest of this page had collapsed. "Across a large set of hands" is two claims, not one, and they have different gates:

Conditioning is not ranking. A design-conditioned policy has to control each hand; it never has to say which hand is better. So a residual that serves a whole family of hands is not blocked by the ranking failure. Only choosing a new hand to build is. The earlier summary on this page conflated them and was too pessimistic by one whole claim.

This was scoped in 2026 and never executed. docs/rl/multimorphology.md lists it as Path C — explicit 9-dim morphology obs, cost "Low", risk "network may not learn to use it." Path A (eval-and-finetune) was chosen instead. Nothing named morph_vec, design_vec or embodiment exists anywhere in src/. The design vector itself is well-defined and already threaded through the generators and the design table.

Payoff 4 is the one that is useful before anything is trusted

The critic's rankings inherit the simulator's contact model and should not be believed. But "which parameter does the task care about" is a much weaker claim than "which design is best," and it is one we can independently check. The design search found that tau_thumb — the thumb's moment arm — carries the result (rho +0.443, AUC 0.821), and that --thumb-axial alone takes rv04_mid from 0.000 to 0.972. If the critic's gradient independently recovers thumb moment arm as the dominant direction, that is real cross-validation of a hand-derived heuristic. If it does not, that is equally informative. Either way it costs one gradient evaluation against a hypothesis that already exists.

Bohlinger's own limitation is in our favour

They report that the full 50-robot set is dominated by quadrupeds and bipeds, and that performance drops on morphologically distinct robots trained on the mixed distribution — morphology-specific training does better. We have one topology and nine continuous parameters. That is squarely the regime they say works. It also means we should expect conditioning to be easier than their result, and the search itself to be less valuable: VGDS solves searching 1177 parameters without a retrain, and we have nine parameters and roughly 117 already-scored designs. Search efficiency is not our bottleneck.

The ablation that must gate this whole line

Our own note against Path C — "high-dim conditioning often gets ignored" — is a live risk with precedent: b33 ignores its observations entirely, and replaying the whole 66-dim input costs nothing. Nine conditioning dims among sixty-six are easy to ignore. So the first test is not performance, it is whether the policy's behaviour changes when only the design vector changes. If it does not, the critic's design gradient is meaningless and every payoff above evaporates. Run that ablation before building anything on top.

What Yang and DASH contribute that VGDS does not

VGDS is a simulation method and inherits every simulation problem. The other two papers are about getting hardware into the loop, which is our actual gate.

Yang et al. built a physically reconfigurable hand with 37 adjustable parameters specifically so evolved designs could be realised and tested. Ours is reconfigurable too, and there is a fact about it that is under-exploited: the finger mounts are on gantries, and apply_mounts is a motorised station operation. Changing the morphology is a stepper move, not a rebuild.

Mannam and Pollard validated a simulation trend against 900+ real trials, and got that number by using teleoperation — taking the per-design policy out of the real-world loop. Our analogue already exists: the open-loop plan is exactly a per-design controller that needs no training. Put those two together and the n=7 gate looks less like a research problem and more like a session-design problem:

The cheapest attack on n = 7

A bench session that batches designs under software control — apply mounts, run the exported plan, log the tag track, apply the next design's mounts — without a human rebuilding the hand between designs. The per-design cost then is staging the object and the operator's attention, not the hardware. Every existing piece (mount application, plan export, clearance gate, tag tracking, per-run logging) is already built; nothing here is new capability, only new sequencing. This is the item that most directly buys back the ability to make any claim about design ranking.

Prior art warning on the DR items

Compliance DR has failed twice in this program, and the recorded verdict is that DR relocates a policy to its band's middle rather than broadening it. What is different now, and the reason it is listed rather than excluded: both prior attempts randomized around an uncalibrated centre on the m05/inline topology. We now have a measured centre (kp 0.5) and a measured band (holds from about 0.3 to 2). If this fails a third time, the honest conclusion is that DR is not the tool, and it should be recorded as closed rather than retried.

Yes — the per-hand residual, concretely

what to build, what to gate it on, and what a bench day looks like

Stated plainly, because it is the near-term question: a residual policy on one hand morphology can be built and trained today, with no hardware time and no new measurement. Everything below is written so it can be picked up cold.

1. Train — sim only, nothing gated

Two traps, both already paid for once: the scene keyframe puts palm_pz at 0.01 against the plan's 0.1035, so a harness that uses it never lifts; and the chord and the CSV are different paths that the clearance gate passes separately — a ranking attempt was voided for replaying the wrong one.

Reward shaping is nearly free here: the residual does not have to discover the turn, which the plan already performs. It has to notice slip. The six bench drops define the target exactly — peak +0.608 reached, then collapse to +0.109.

2. The gate that does not exist yet

Owed before any residual touches hardware

Every safety gate this program has — real_v1_trajectory_clearance.py (finger interpenetration) and the servo-limit check (family clip ceiling about 1.34 rad, lower for hands leaning on middle yaw) — validates a fixed trajectory. A residual policy by definition does not have one: it moves the path at run time. Clearing the base path is therefore not sufficient, and no existing gate covers this.

What is needed is an envelope gate: sample the trained residual over its action bound and reset distribution, take the swept commanded envelope, and run the existing two gates on that rather than on the base path. Three of the four plans sitting on the CB1 in 2026-09 were ones the clearance gate had excluded; this is not a hypothetical failure mode.

3. Data collection — what a run must record

Nothing new needs building; the point is to say what must be on before the first trial, because a run missing any of it is not interpretable and cannot be repeated.

  • Tag track — the object-pose channel, and the only ground truth for whether the turn happened. The tracker must be launched so BENCH_DEFAULTS apply (--shaft-axis=-x --tag-end top --symmetric-object --heading-deg 90 --exposure 4000 --gain 64). A session was lost to launching it bare, and a run was misread as "no rotation" for want of --symmetric-object, which folds cos to [0,1].
  • Servo load and temperature, per run. Load is the only force proxy on this part and is what shows a lost object; protective_torque fires on 42% of middle_yaw bench runs and doubles both deflection and scatter, so a trial without temperature cannot be told apart from one that tripped.
  • The commanded stream, frame by frame, alongside the base trajectory — the residual is the difference, and without both there is nothing to compare against the sim.
  • The station's own run log, which already captures commands and telemetry phases.

One thing to confirm at the bench rather than assume: the tag update rate reaching the station. The policy's feedback bandwidth is bounded by it, and it is the one number in this loop that has never been measured under load.

4. Deployment sequence

Pre-flight, on the workstation, before a bench day starts:

# base path must clear BOTH the chord and the per-step CSV
uv run python scripts/real_v1_trajectory_clearance.py --plan <design>
# then the same two gates on the residual's swept envelope  (TO BUILD)
# then a corrected-plant retention check, the shape of plant_drop_gate.py

At the bench, in this order:

  1. Workstation: tracker service under ~/miniconda3/bin/python — the camera stack is not in the uv env. Launch it plain so its defaults apply.
  2. CB1: ~/run_control_station.sh start, which carries the token and --tracker-url. Without that URL /reorient silently runs untracked trials.
  3. Apply the design's mounts; stage the object in the design's open-grasp keyframe.
  4. Grasp. Confirm with the operator before the reorient — the object is staged in a holder that fights the turn, and the holder is pulled between the two.
  5. Stream the residual. No joint feedback is available during a stream; the policy sees the object and its own commands.

Both serial ports are exclusive: stop the station before any raw-bus work, and remember that telemetry suspends while a writer owns the bus, so a probe holding the stream open reads one stale sample repeated.

What this would and would not prove

A residual trained on a plant calibrated to one hand, deployed on that hand, that recovers turns the open-loop plan loses to slip — that is a clean, defensible first closed-loop sim2real result, and it is the first one this program would have. It says nothing about other morphologies until the design-conditioned version above is trained, and nothing about design ranking at all.

The honest summary

A residual policy for reorientation on a specific hand is not blocked by anything physical. The plant is measured and validated, the residual machinery exists, the observation the policy needs is available off the servo bus during a stream, and the failure mode it would fix — a turn that is won and then lost to slip — is documented in six bench drops. That work can proceed with no hardware time.

A residual that controls a family of hands is also not blocked, once conditioning is separated from ranking. It needs the 9-dim design vector in the observation and critic, scoped as Path C in docs/rl/multimorphology.md and never built, and it pays four ways: the multi-hand residual itself, near-free per-design evaluation, the frozen critic that VGDS needs, and a gradient diagnostic over the nine design parameters that can be checked against the thumb-moment-arm result we already have. One ablation gates all of it: does the policy behave differently when only the design vector changes.

Choosing a hand is what is blocked, and not on anything that can be simulated away. The simulator does not order hands the way the bench does, before or after the actuator correction; the missing physics is contact; and both identifying it and verifying any fix require bench measurements we do not have. Seven designs is the ceiling, and it is a low one. The cheapest attack on it is not a better algorithm but a bench session that batches designs under software control: the mounts are motorised, so changing morphology is a stepper move.
