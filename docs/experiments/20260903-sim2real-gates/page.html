Gates on a Deployable Residual

A standing account of what now blocks a sim2real residual reorientation policy, written for a reader with no hardware time. The short answer is that the question splits in two: a residual on one hand is not blocked by anything physical and is mostly a base-schedule swap; a residual across a set of hands is blocked, and the binding resource is not compute or algorithms but bench measurements.

The question splits in two, and only one half is blocked

"a residual RL policy for reorientation across a large set of hands"

These are separate claims with separate evidence and separate blockers, and conflating them is what makes the program look more stuck than it is.

Claim A is not gated on hardware. The actuator model is measured, corrected and validated against the bench on the one hand we have most data for. What remains is software that can be written today.

Claim B is gated, and the gate is not "we need a better search algorithm." It is that the simulator does not currently order hands the way the bench orders them, and the measurement we would need to fix that — and, more importantly, to verify any fix — only exists on the bench.

What the plant work settled

2026-09-02, 236 archived runs — full detail in the sysid page

The servo is a spring with a cliff. Deflection is 0.0186° per unit of load, one constant for all nine joints, R² 0.94–0.99, no meaningful intercept. The mechanism is in the register file: p=15, i=0, d=15 — with no integral term a standing load leaves a proportional error forever. The cliff is protective_torque, which fires on 42% of middle_yaw bench runs.

Correcting the simulator means finger gain kp 30 → 0.5 (about 60× softer than shipped), forcerange 0.35, frictionloss 0.0035, and real link masses. On rv05 that closes the gap on both axes at once, where the shipped plant closed neither:

The cross-validation is the part worth trusting: kp was fitted to joint angles alone, and it independently lands where the whole-task outcome matches. Compliance turns out to be the carry mechanism, which is why the stiff shipped plant under-turns.

The limit of that result

It is a result about one hand's dynamics. It says the simulator can be made to reproduce rv05. It does not say the simulator can be made to compare hands, and the next section is the direct test of that.

The correction lifts the level. It does not fix the ordering.

seven designs, six trials each, replaying the exported per-step trajectory

Spearman against the bench moves −0.536 → +0.107 (n=7); over the five designs that run in both plants it is −0.300. Three designs land within about 0.1 of the bench. Four do not.

Both worst misses are slip cases, and they fail in opposite directions. sv1_u1364 is the design whose bench trials are not a distribution but a mixture — 0.21, 0.30, 0.32, 0.33, 0.61, 0.66, 0.70, 0.78, sd 0.221, roughly four times any other design's, from trials that held their grip and trials that slipped partway. The corrected simulator gives it 0.920 and retains every trial: it does not reproduce the slip at all. sv1_w0099 is the mirror — one of only two designs that dropped on the bench, and the corrected plant now drops it on every trial instead of some.

Do not compare this to +0.36

The transfer first pass reported spearman +0.36 (p = 0.43) on these same seven designs. That number came from a different harness — real_v1_deploy_envelope's rebuild with its own retention rule. This harness replays the exported trajectory, and its shipped baseline is −0.536, not +0.36. Only shipped-vs-corrected within one harness is a valid comparison. Both harnesses agree on the thing that matters: no usable relationship.

Two independent routes point at contact

and neither can be followed without the bench

The ranking failure is one route. The hysteresis measurement is the other, and it arrives at the same place from the opposite side.

A free-hanging finger's staircase shows a friction loop of 0.70–1.50°. Under a grasp, walking the plan's own chord forward and back, middle_yaw's loop is +12.26° mean, +14.06° max — about eleven times wider, while the load range grew only about fivefold. That excess cannot be attributed to the gearbox with the data in hand, because middle_pip shows an 8.16° loop at a load of only 109, which does not fit a friction model. The obvious remaining explanation is the object moving.

So the two routes agree: what the simulator is missing is contact, not actuator. And identifying contact means measuring slip, which means the hand.

Consequence, recorded deliberately

No further design search should be run against this simulator expecting it to rank hands. Neither plant ranks them. A faster or better-guided search over an objective with no demonstrated relationship to the bench converges more efficiently on a hand we cannot trust.

The hard gate is n = 7

the ceiling on every sim-fidelity claim this program can currently make

The entire bench validation set is 77 trials over 8 morphologies — 52 held, 6 dropped, 19 unresolved — of which 7 designs have a usable cos_hold. One of those seven (sv1_u1364) is a mixture rather than a distribution, and one more (g12) is 10/10 unresolved and dropped entirely.

This caps what can be concluded regardless of how much simulation is run. A spearman on n=7 has a 95% confidence interval spanning most of the achievable range; the transfer first pass's own p-value was 0.43. Any improvement to the contact model would have to be validated against seven numbers, and seven numbers cannot distinguish a real improvement from a reshuffle.

This is the sense in which the program is genuinely gated on physical testing, and it is worth stating plainly: more bench designs is the limiting resource for sim2real fidelity work, and no amount of GPU time substitutes for it.

What is not gated: the residual is a base-schedule swap

the machinery already exists; it is pointed at the wrong base

This was worth checking rather than assuming, and the finding is favourable. The action term in src/morphohand/rl/actions.py is already a bounded finger residual on top of a scripted set-point schedule, with a residual_active_from_sim_step gate for exactly the "let the script run, then let the policy correct" pattern. Its own docstring describes the reorient as a residual problem.

What it lacks is that its base schedule is a training-time scripted close, not the exported deployment plan. The three concrete pieces of work:

  • Reset from meta.replay_base_ctrl rather than the scene keyframe. This is already known to be load-bearing — the plant gate found the keyframe puts palm_pz at 0.01 against the plan's 0.1035, so a harness using the keyframe never lifts at all.
  • Drive the base from the plan's per-step _traj.csv, not the three-set-point chord. These are different paths — the clearance gate passes them separately, and a first ranking attempt was voided for replaying the chord.
  • Train on the corrected plant, with the residual clip stated as a maneuver-specific choice rather than inherited. The 0.5 rad figure was Policy B's action budget and has been hardcoded through every screen since; it helps the bench hold and hurts the proof-lift retention gate.

None of that needs the hand. It produces a residual for rv05 (or any hand with an exported plan), trained against a plant that demonstrably matches that hand on retention and alignment. That is a defensible deliverable on its own, and it is the cheapest real progress available right now.

What it would not be is a claim about transfer across designs. Stated as "a residual trained on a calibrated plant for a specific hand," it is honest and useful. Stated as "a residual that generalizes across morphologies," it is not supported.

What the deploy path can carry today

read before designing the policy's observation vector

The streaming endpoints exist (/api/v1/stream/{start,frame,end}), and the runtime announces the stream as "experimental write-only policy stream armed." That phrasing is exact. In the telemetry loop:

include_servos = idle or (not self._servo_writing.is_set()
                          and self._operation != "policy stream")

During a policy stream the runtime never reads the servos. A residual deployed on this path today gets no joint feedback. That is a deliberate half-duplex bus decision, not an oversight.

The important corollary is that this does not block a closed loop, because the object-pose sensor is not on that bus. The AprilTag tracker runs on the workstation and pushes already-reduced geometry to the station over the network, at 0.017° / 0.03 mm rms. So:

ObservationHardware sourceAvailable during a stream?
object pose / orientationAprilTags, over the networkyes — off the servo bus
joint positionsservo telemetry, half-duplex busno — suppressed by design

An object-only residual is deployable on the path that already exists. This suits the evidence: b33 ignores its observations, and replaying the whole 66-dim input costs nothing. It also suits the failure mode the residual is meant to catch — the six bench drops all turn the shaft to a mean peak of +0.608, which is 37° of the 90° turn already won, and then collapse to +0.109. The residual does not need to discover the turn. It needs to notice the shaft escaping, which is precisely what the tags see and the open-loop plan is blind to.

Joint feedback in the loop is a separate, genuinely hardware-gated question: whether reads and writes can interleave on the half-duplex bus at 50 Hz. Sync writes are known not to be rate-limiting and reads have been clocked at 111 Hz, but interleaved at rate is unmeasured.

On sampling and ranking hands: the objective, not the algorithm

responding to the gradient-guided search question

The instinct is right that scoring-then-sorting is a weak way to search, and the literature in relevant_papers/ supports the direction. But the ordering of the fixes matters, and right now the search method is not the binding constraint.

A gradient tells you which way to move on the objective you have. Our objective is a simulator whose rank correlation with the bench is +0.107. Backpropagating through it moves faster along a direction we have not shown points anywhere. That is the ranking result's direct consequence, and it applies to any search method, gradient or otherwise.

What the three papers actually offer

PaperMethodBearing on us

Yang et al., RA-L 2024		 "Hardware gradients" + RL in parallel envs, 37 variable hardware parameters on a physically reconfigurable hand Closest to the described idea, and unusually applicable — ours is also a physically reconfigurable hand via the gantry mounts. This is probably the one you meant.
pollard_optimization.pdf
Mannam, Liu, Zhao, Oh, Pollard 2024		 GA + policy transfer across ~400 designs; validated with 900+ teleoperated real tasks The relevant one for our actual problem. Their headline claim is exactly ours: "the trend in design performance in simulation resembles that of the real world." They earned it with real-world validation at scale. We have n=7 and the trend does not resemble.
diffmjx.pdf
Paulus, Geist et al., arXiv:2506.14186		 Soft gradients through hard contacts, corrections applied in the backward pass only The enabling technology for literal backprop-through-sim. Note the practical blockers already recorded in docs/notes/diffmjx.md: the code is not public, and it targets MJX rather than MuJoCo-Warp.

The affordable version we have not done

Mannam's actual lever is not the genetic algorithm, it is policy transfer — and we have independently measured why that works. From-scratch reorient training has a between-draw sd of 0.3–0.5, which is why single-draw design rankings were noise and why the program costed a design search at n=48 replicates. Warmstarted, the between-draw sd is 0.032 — smaller than the 0.080 rollout noise. About two seeds then resolve a Δ of 0.1.

That makes genuine policy-in-the-loop ranking affordable today, with no gradients and no new simulator. It is roughly a 20× reduction in the cost of evaluating a design properly, and it is the single biggest unexploited lever in the search program.

The caveat is the same one as everywhere else on this page, and it is why this is listed as a lever rather than a plan: it makes the sim ranking cheap, not correct. Policy-in-the-loop evaluation resolves differences between designs faithfully in simulation. Whether those differences survive to the bench is the n=7 question.

The order these want to be done in

1. Make the evaluation trustworthy per design (warmstarted policy-in-the-loop — cheap, sim-only, available now). 2. Establish that sim differences predict bench differences (needs bench designs). 3. Then make the search efficient (gradients, GA, transfer). Doing 3 before 2 is the failure this page exists to prevent.

Prior art warning on the DR items

Compliance DR has failed twice in this program, and the recorded verdict is that DR relocates a policy to its band's middle rather than broadening it. What is different now, and the reason it is listed rather than excluded: both prior attempts randomized around an uncalibrated centre on the m05/inline topology. We now have a measured centre (kp 0.5) and a measured band (holds from about 0.3 to 2). If this fails a third time, the honest conclusion is that DR is not the tool, and it should be recorded as closed rather than retried.

Needs a bench campaign — and this is the real gate

Expanding the validation set beyond seven designs. Everything about ranking, transfer, design search and contact identification terminates here. It is the one item that no amount of simulation, compute or algorithmic work can substitute for, and it should be planned as a measurement campaign rather than folded into another study.

The honest summary

A residual policy for reorientation on a specific hand is not blocked by anything physical. The plant is measured and validated, the residual machinery exists, the observation the policy needs is available off the servo bus during a stream, and the failure mode it would fix — a turn that is won and then lost to slip — is documented in six bench drops. That work can proceed with no hardware time.

A residual policy across a large set of hands is blocked, and not on anything that can be simulated away. The simulator does not order hands the way the bench does, before or after the actuator correction; the missing physics is contact; and both identifying it and verifying any fix require bench measurements we do not have. Seven designs is the ceiling, and it is a low one.

The recommendation, stated once: build the per-hand residual now and claim exactly that, and treat the multi-hand ranking claim as owing a measurement campaign rather than an algorithm.
