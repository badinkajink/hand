Training throughput on the workstation and a DeltaAI GH200

A GH200 compute node runs the MuJoCo-Warp training loop at 0.74× the workstation’s rate, measured against the workstation’s whole logged population rather than its best hour. Over the 50 days that population covers, the workstation’s GPU was busy 9.7% of the time, which places the binding constraint somewhere other than throughput.

214 trainer logs · 69,333 PPO iterations · 3.93 G env-steps · 116.7 GPU-hours · 2026-07-10 to 2026-08-28. GH200 figures are transcribed from jobs 2989123 and 2989164 and are not reproducible from this machine.

What each machine delivers per GPU

logs/*.log, steady-state iterations · docs/notes/20260820-deltaai_bulk_training_runbook.md

Every trainer log in logs/ that got past five PPO iterations reports its own steps/s, collection time and learning time each iteration. 214 logs qualify. At 3072 parallel environments the median run sustains 9,417 env-steps/s (interquartile range 9,298 to 9,505, n = 86); at 2048 it sustains 9,392 (n = 127). A single GH200 job at the same batch width reached 6,795.

Correction to the recorded ratio

The 0.66× figure in the runbook and in docs/notes/20260826-nsf_access_deltaai_guide.md was computed against a local reference of 10,530 steps/s taken from one run on 2026-08-19. That number does not appear anywhere in the population: the fastest of the 86 logged runs at 3072 envs reached 10,310, and the median is 9,417. Against the median the GH200 is 0.74×, not 0.66×. The direction of the finding is unchanged and the sizing tables built on it are conservative by about 11%.

Where the wall clock goes

median over 214 logs, iteration 0 excluded

Collection takes 98.68% of an iteration and the PPO update takes 1.32%; the worst learning share in the whole population is 1.85%. Iteration time minus collection minus learning leaves 0.01% unaccounted, so there is no hidden third term. Every learning-side knob — epochs, minibatch count, learning rate, network width — divides a budget of one and a third percent.

Batch width does not help either, and the reason is visible in the per-env-step cost. One iteration simulates num_envs × 24 env-steps, so dividing collection time by that gives a cost per simulated step that is free of batch size. On the workstation it is 105.57 µs at 1024 envs, 105.00 µs at 2048 and 104.99 µs at 3072 — a spread of 0.01% across a threefold change in batch. The GH200 behaves the same way at its own level, 141.7 µs from 8192 to 16384.

A workload whose per-item cost is invariant to batch width is not throughput-limited. It is limited by the depth of the dependency chain inside one step: MuJoCo's solver runs iterations × ls_iterations sequential passes, and with 15 joints and a handful of contacts none of those passes is wide enough to fill the device. That matches what the card reports while training, 43–46% utilisation with CUDA graph capture already enabled, and it explains why the two levers people reach for first are both spent: the solver already ships at iterations=10, ls_iterations=20 (set in env_build.py, overriding whatever the scene XML says), and adding environments widens kernels that were never the constraint.

Why the GH200 loses on a workload it should win

clock ratio 1.42× against measured cost ratio 1.35×

A latency-bound workload spends its time waiting on a chain of dependent operations, so its wall clock scales with clock period rather than with FLOPs or bandwidth. That predicts the deficit directly. The workstation's SM clock reads 2,820 MHz under load (nvidia-smi --query-gpu=clocks.sm, 2026-09-08, ceiling 3,120); the GH200's Hopper die boosts to a specified 1,980 MHz. The ratio is 1.42×. The measured per-env-step cost ratio is 1.35×. Those agree to 5%.

Read that way the result stops being a surprise. The GH200 brings roughly twice the SMs and six times the memory, and this workload uses neither: it needs one narrow kernel to finish so the next can start. The estimate that predicted 1–2.5× faster was reading the specification sheet in the units of a dense-matmul workload.

Sizing rule for this stack

Rank candidate GPUs for mjwarp training by clock, not by FLOPs, memory or SM count, and confirm with a smoke job on a real scene before committing an allocation. A consumer part with a high boost clock will beat a datacenter part at the same generation on this workload, and the gap does not close at any batch size either machine can hold.

The clock figure for the GH200 is a specification, not a measurement — nobody has read clocks.sm on the compute node under load. That measurement is listed at the end.

The workstation GPU is idle nine days in ten

116.7 GPU-hours over 50 calendar days

The 214 logs sum to 116.7 hours of training spread across 50 days, a duty cycle of 9.7%. 14 days carried a run and 36 did not. On the days it ran at all the GPU averaged 8.3 hours, so when work exists the machine is used hard; the gaps are between studies, not inside them.

This reframes what extra compute can buy. Adding a second GPU-equivalent to a machine running at 9.7% occupancy shortens nothing that was waiting on a GPU, because almost nothing was. The 36 idle days went to rendering filmstrips, reading trajectory health scorecards, bench sessions on the CB1, and deciding what the last result meant. Those are the serial resource.

Two caveats on the number. It counts only runs that logged five or more iterations, so crashed launches and short probes are missing, and it counts only RL training — CEM grasp synthesis, evaluation sweeps and rendering also use the card and are not in logs/*.log. The true occupancy is higher than 9.7%; it is not close to 100%.

The first cluster study removed the reason for the cluster

docs/experiments/B33_SEED_BAND.txt · 16 draws, 12.8 GPU-hours, 15 SU

The allocation was sized around one quantity: the standard deviation between independent training draws of the same design. The program-wide prior was 0.35, taken from from-scratch reorient training, and at that value separating two designs by 0.2 in mean held-cos needs 48 seeds each. Twenty designs at that resolution is roughly 830 GPU-hours, which is what the whole allocation was for.

The seed-band study ran 16 draws of b33's lineage on DeltaAI for 15 SU. The 15 core draws span 0.827 to 0.935 with a between-draw sd of 0.032 (0.11 including the one drop-prone tail draw), against a within-draw rollout sd of 0.08. The training draw is less variable than the evaluation noise. The 0.35 prior belongs to from-scratch training of a hard-exploration objective; warmstarting from the design's own Policy A removes the lottery.

At the measured sd, the 20-design comparison that justified the allocation costs 16 GH200-hours, or under twelve hours on the workstation. The study answered its own question and priced its own successor out of needing a cluster. It also cost 15 SU and produced the second result the program needed: b33 sits at 0.891 with 11/16 at or below draws, so the sim-to-real hardware spec downstream of it rests on a representative policy rather than a lucky one.

One limit on how far that generalises. The seed band shared a single Policy A warmstart across all 16 draws. Whether sd 0.032 survives per-design from-scratch A training is untested, and a per-design search cannot reuse one hand's A on another's geometry — that substitution is what NaN'd the first attempt, 15 of 16 tasks diverging.

The timestep budget is a larger lever than the hardware

b33's 20M-timestep run, docs/rl/reorientation.md

Two budgets dominate the logged population: 125 runs at 30M timesteps and 76 at 20M. b33's own 20M run converged at iteration 26 of 271.

Cutting a converged run to its convergence point is worth about 10×. The best available hardware move, running on a GH200 instead, is 0.74× in the wrong direction. Any plan that adds compute before trimming the budget is buying the smaller lever at the higher price. The trimming is not free of risk — a shorter budget on a from-scratch run is a different proposition than on a warmstarted finetune, and gotcha 13 applies, since the recipes carry reward shaping but not the timestep budget, so cutting it means editing every launcher that passes --total-timesteps explicitly.

What the remaining 92 SU are worth

balance 92 of 100 deposited hours, 2026-08-20

The one study actually run gives the honest wall-clock arithmetic. Sixteen draws of 20M timesteps is 9.4 hours of exclusive GPU on the workstation, run end to end. On DeltaAI at 16-way concurrency and zero queue wait it is 0.8 hours, a 12× improvement; at 8-way it is 1.6 hours and 5.9×. The write-up records the actual elapsed time as “a few hours”, which means queue wait, not throughput, set the delivered result. Nobody measured it.

Against that the accounting is straightforward. The study consumed 12.8 GPU-hours of work and was billed 15 SU, an overhead of 1.17× from reservation rounding. The entire 50-day local training history — 3.93 G env-steps, 116 hours here — would take 157 GH200-hours, which does not fit in the 92 SU on the account.

Where the allocation still pays

Work that is genuinely wide and that nobody is waiting on: seed bands, hyperparameter or reward-shaping sweeps, ablations across many hands, and any question of the form “is this effect larger than the draw-to-draw noise”. The seed band is the model case. It was 9.4 hours of exclusive local GPU on a machine with one card, which is why it went unasked for two months, and it settled a question the whole sim-to-real spec depended on for 15 SU.

Where it does not

Anything with a person at the end of it. Per-run latency is worse, so a single run being watched belongs here. The A→B pipeline stays sequential per design because B warmstarts from A's checkpoint, so parallelism is across designs and seeds only, and chaining wants --dependency=afterok rather than lifting morph_pipeline_sweep.py onto a compute node. Rendering is worse than not cluster-shaped: EGL on the node image is untested and video encoding is billed GPU time, so filmstrips and eval videos come back to the workstation. And bulk training raises the risk the program is already most exposed to, since 48 seeds produce 48 reward curves and no pictures, while every conclusion in this repo has needed someone to look at a rollout.

What this does not settle

  1. Queue wait is unmeasured, and it decides the previous section. The 12× figure assumes 16 GPUs start at once. Run sacct -j <jobid> --format=JobID,Submit,Start,Elapsed,Reserved,State after the next queue and take the median Submit→Start delta. If it exceeds about 9.4 hours, breadth on the cluster is slower in wall clock than running the same work serially here.
  2. The GH200's clock under load has never been read. Add nvidia-smi --query-gpu=clocks.sm,clocks.max.sm,power.draw,utilization.gpu --format=csv -l 10 to the next deltaai_smoke.slurm. A reading near 1,980 MHz confirms the latency explanation; a reading near 2,820 MHz falsifies it and sends the deficit back to memory subsystem or kernel dispatch.
  3. Billing granularity is still unresolved. The evidence is mixed: two short jobs moved the balance 92 → 91 → 91, while 13.9 hours of packed work drew 15 SU. Run one 8-hour packed block, wait a day, and re-read accounts. If charging is per reserved hour the packed queue in deltaai_pack_queue.slurm is correct and job arrays waste a tail per task; if it is per elapsed second the array is simpler and equally cheap.
  4. The convergence point is n = 1. 26-of-271 comes from one run. Parse Metrics/.../target_axis_alignment out of the tensorboard logs of the 76 runs at that budget and report the first iteration within 2% of each run's final value. A median convergence point above iteration 100 would make the trimming argument much weaker than it looks here.
  5. Whether the draw-to-draw sd holds without a shared warmstart. 0.032 was measured with all 16 draws warmstarted from the same a10 checkpoint. The test is a two-stage A-then-B queue on one hand, n = 4 from-scratch A per design, and it is the number that decides whether a per-design morphology search is affordable at all.
  6. The duty cycle is a floor, not a measurement of the card. It counts RL training logs only. Logging wall-clock GPU occupancy directly — nvidia-smi --query-gpu=utilization.gpu --format=csv -l 60 to a rotating file — would show how much of the 36 idle days is CEM, evaluation and rendering rather than genuinely idle.
