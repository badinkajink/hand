Reinforcement-learning policies for the screwdriver turn

No learned policy has run on the hand; the bench results use the open-loop plan. A policy adds a learned correction to a scripted lift and turn and is trained with PPO in MuJoCo-Warp at about 9,400 environment steps per second. On a servo plant limited to the SCS0009’s torque (0.18 N·m), D6 policies grip the tool with 13–18 N, and those trained with compliant sphere-pad fingertips turn it nearly as far as those trained with point contact (held cosine 0.79–0.88 against 0.85–0.90); in open-loop replays in CPU MuJoCo, Drake and Newton the pad commands keep the tool in 78 of 88 and the point-contact commands in 74 of 88. On the earlier working plant, whose 1 N·m torque limit is 4.4 times the servo’s stall torque, the pad policies gripped with 44–55 N and turned the tool less (0.61–0.69). Policies trained on fingers that lagged by 1 s drop the tool at bench finger speed.

Terms

Cosine
Cosine of the tool axis with vertical: +1 tip down (the goal), 0 horizontal; held cosine is its mean over the rollouts that hold the tool.
Held
At the end of the episode at least two fingertips press on the tool with its weight, 0.240 N, or more, and the tool is above 60 mm.
Residual
The policy’s nine finger actions, 0.5 rad per unit, added to set-points that ease from the open keyframe to the grip; the palm motion is scripted.
Plant
The simulated servo model: shipped (gain 30 N·m/rad), calibrated (0.5, 1.04 s finger lag), working (4 N·m/rad, 0.02 s lag, fitted to 177 bench runs, torque limit 1 N·m), servo-limited (0.72 N·m/rad, 0.02 s, torque limit 0.18 N·m, 80 % of the SCS0009’s stall torque).
Jittered
The same 64 evaluation rollouts with the tool placed ±3 mm and ±10° off the trained pose and the friction randomised.
Open-loop replay
The finger targets recorded from a MuJoCo-Warp rollout, played at 50 Hz into another simulator with the palm held at its lifted pose.
Environment step
One 20 ms policy step of one simulated hand: ten 2 ms physics steps and the reward and observation code.

Policy structure and training

A policy outputs nine finger corrections every 20 ms on top of a scripted episode: the fingers ease from the open keyframe to the grip, the palm lifts the tool, and the policy acts from step 58 for the turn and the hold. The policy reads 66 numbers: positions and velocities of the 15 hand joints, the tool’s position and pose in the palm frame, the finger joints and tool pose of the open-loop reference rollout at the same step, the previous action and , where is the cosine. Actor and critic are separate 512-256-128 ELU networks; a warm start copies both, because actor-only warm starts pushed converged policies off their optimum. From step 58 the reward pays for alignment and per step for progress, with tracking and contact terms throughout, a lift term during the lift, and penalties on drift, action rate and pad force above a threshold. An episode ends when the tool falls 20 mm below its spawn height or fewer than three pads touch it for 15 consecutive steps (policies continued on the chain plant). PPO trains 2,048–3,072 hands in parallel at about 9,400 environment steps per second on the workstation’s GPU, independent of the batch width; a DeltaAI GH200 runs at 0.74 of that speed, and 40 M steps cost 1.2–3.4 GPU-hours (training throughput, contact-model policies).

The scripted lift replaced a learned one. On m05, the earlier simulation-only hand, a lift policy (A) and a turn policy (B) were trained separately because the two rewards conflicted, and every B trained from a teleported, pre-lifted tool dropped it at the hand-off. The live-A reset (src/morphohand/rl/live_a_runner.py) lets a frozen A drive the first 40 steps of each B episode and zeroes those steps’ advantages; B then held the tool at 0.110 m, and the reference turn policy b33 reached a cosine of 0.891. Recipes in configs/recipes/ fix the residual scale, easing and contact gate for training and evaluation alike; on real_v1 a script performs the lift, and the recipe keeps the name b_liveA (program record).

Servo plants

Four servo plants have been used. Policies up to 2026-09-16 trained on the shipped plant. The D6 and D1–D7 policies of 2026-09-17 to 09-20 trained on the calibrated plant, whose fingers lagged by 1.04 s because a template joint damping stayed in the model: D6 reached a cosine of 0.969 in 64 of 64 rollouts at a grip of 10.8 N (D6 at 60 M steps), but at bench finger speed none of ten policies holds the tool through its turn (chain with the learned turn). The policies of 2026-10-08 train on the working plant (4 N·m/rad, 0.02 s), fitted to the finger angles of bench runs; its torque limit of 1 N·m is 4.4 times the SCS0009’s stall torque of 0.23 N·m, and its policies grip the 24.5 g tool with 44–96 N. The policies of 2026-10-11 train on the servo-limited plant: 0.18 N·m, where the servo’s overload protection trips, and 0.72 N·m/rad, the gain at which its drive saturates at 18° of error (servo-plant policies).

Seeds and evaluation

Policy quality depends on the training draw more than on the recipe. Five from-scratch draws on m05, the earlier simulation-only hand, spread their held cosine with a standard deviation of 0.38; sixteen draws there warm-started from one policy spread by 0.032; two from-scratch D6 runs of 60 M steps ended at 0.933 and 0.640 (training throughput, D6 at 60 M steps). A design comparison therefore needs several seeds or a shared warm start.

The trajectory-health scorecard (src/morphohand/rl/trajectory_health.py) grades a rollout on six checks (late finger, idle finger, drop, jitter, de-centring, over-clamp) and reports the worst. The checkpoint watch of the 2026-10-08 runs evaluates a run every 4 M steps and stops it after 10 M steps of degenerate checkpoints or when its held cosine gains less than 0.02 over 10 M steps (contact-model policies).

A policy is judged on jittered rollouts with a load test, under its own run’s timing. Evaluated with the residual switched on too early, the D6 policy held 12 of 64 rollouts instead of 64. The policies of 2026-09-19 hold 44–64 of 64 jittered rollouts on the plant they trained on, and with the tool on the scene’s contact class 11 of 16 drop it even at the trained pose (D7 0 of 64); continued on that class with jitter, D5 holds 55 and D7 28 of 64 jittered rollouts. Evaluations store their per-step traces beside their pages (traces/). Without a bound the residual commanded up to 384° past a joint’s range; clipped at ±1 rad the policies still hold 64 of 64 at the trained pose (policies across the hands, policies continued).

The engineering log is markdown in docs/rl/ (reorientation.md lists the runs in order); the autopilot hand-off notes are in docs/handoff/.

Fingertip contact model and transfer

Twelve D6 runs on the working plant compared four fingertip models, three seeds each, 40 M steps under a checkpoint watch that stops degenerate runs. Point-contact policies turn the tool further where they hold (cosine 0.84–0.94 against 0.61–0.69 for the pads) but shake it at 302 and 126 rad/s² against 18, and five of their six runs degenerated. Their finger commands keep the tool in 3 of 132 replays in other simulators and the pad commands in 85 of 88, and in closed loop under the other contact models the pad policies hold 76 % of rollouts. Every final policy grips with 40–100 N, and all but one lose the tool when the finger servo gain rises to 10 N·m/rad (contact-model policies). The fourth arm, the same pads mounted on a sprung skin, held 99 % in closed loop. It trained at the same grips and at impratio 10, where the skin’s springs store half of what its friction rows creep, so its policies did not use the presliding the skin models; that arm is reported with the skin (sprung skin).

On the servo-limited plant, with a penalty in place of the bonus on fingertip force above 4 N, two pad and two TPU-mesh seeds hold the tool in 256 of 256 rollouts at a grip of 13–18 N. The pad policies turn it nearly as far as the TPU-mesh ones (held cosine 0.79–0.88 against 0.85–0.90), and during the turn the tool spins 97–98° relative to the pads, against 32–52° at the working plant’s grip. Given 1 N·m, pad seed 0 grips with 44 N and stops turning the tool (held cosine 0.02) and pad seed 1 drops 28 of 64 rollouts, while the TPU-mesh policies turn it as before; a pad’s torsional friction grows as the 5/4 power of its force. Both kinds of finger commands transfer: the pad commands keep the tool in 78 of 88 open-loop replays, the TPU-mesh commands in 74 of 88, and Newton loses the most (servo-plant policies).

Future work

  1. Replay the finger commands of the servo-plant pad and TPU-mesh final policies on the bench at 50 Hz from the lifted grasp, after the trajectory-clearance gate, ten tracked trials each (logs/20261011-servo_plant_policies/replay/). Commands that drop the tool on the hand in most trials, where the three simulators keep it, put the gap in the plant or the fingertip model.
  2. Retrain one pad and one TPU-mesh seed with the grip penalty at −20: at −2 the term costs under 1 % of the return and the thumb presses 6–8 N with its mcp at the torque limit; a policy that turns the tool as far with the thumb under 4 N does not need the saturated thumb.
  3. Evaluate the servo-plant policies at torque limits of 0.12 and 0.23 N·m (rl_contact_eval.py robust --study 20261011 --perturb fr=0.12 fr=0.23); a pad policy that stops turning at 0.23 makes the torque limit a parameter to measure on the bench.
  4. Clip the residual (clip_actions 1.0) for one pad and one TPU-mesh seed and compare the held cosine.
