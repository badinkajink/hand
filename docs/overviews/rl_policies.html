Reinforcement-learning policies for the screwdriver turn

No learned policy has run on the hand; the bench results use the open-loop plan. A policy adds a learned correction to a scripted lift and turn and is trained with PPO in MuJoCo-Warp at about 9,400 environment steps per second. On the servo model fitted to the bench, D6 policies trained with compliant sphere-pad fingertips keep the tool in 216 of 220 open-loop replays in CPU MuJoCo, Drake and Newton, and policies trained with point contact in 3 of 132. Earlier policies, trained on fingers that lagged by 1 s, drop the tool at bench finger speed.

Terms

Cosine
Cosine of the tool axis with vertical: +1 tip down (the goal), 0 horizontal; held cosine is its mean over the rollouts that hold the tool.
Held
At the end of the episode at least two fingertips press on the tool with its weight, 0.240 N, or more, and the tool is above 60 mm.
Residual
The policy’s nine finger actions, 0.5 rad per unit, added to set-points that ease from the open keyframe to the grip; the palm motion is scripted.
Plant
The simulated servo model: shipped (gain 30 N·m/rad), calibrated (0.5, 1.04 s finger lag), working (4 N·m/rad, 0.02 s lag, fitted to 177 bench runs).
Jittered
The same 64 evaluation rollouts with the tool placed ±3 mm and ±10° off the trained pose and the friction randomised.
Open-loop replay
The finger targets recorded from a MuJoCo-Warp rollout, played at 50 Hz into another simulator with the palm held at its lifted pose.
Environment step
One 20 ms policy step of one simulated hand: ten 2 ms physics steps and the reward and observation code.

Policy structure and training

A policy outputs nine finger corrections every 20 ms on top of a scripted episode: the fingers ease from the open keyframe to the grip, the palm lifts the tool, and the policy acts from step 58 for the turn and the hold. The reward grows with the tool axis’s alignment with vertical, its progress toward it and pad contact, and falls with lateral drift and grip force above a threshold (policies continued on the chain plant). PPO trains 2,048–3,072 hands in parallel at about 9,400 environment steps per second on the workstation’s GPU, independent of the batch width; a DeltaAI GH200 runs at 0.74 of that speed, and 40 M steps cost 1.2–3.4 GPU-hours (training throughput, contact-model policies).

Servo plants

Three servo plants have been used, and only the last is fitted to bench readings. Policies up to 2026-09-16 trained on the shipped plant. The D6 and D1–D7 policies of 2026-09-17 to 09-20 trained on the calibrated plant, whose fingers lagged by 1.04 s because a template joint damping stayed in the model: D6 reached a cosine of 0.969 in 64 of 64 rollouts at a grip of 10.8 N (D6 at 60 M steps), but at bench finger speed none of ten policies holds the tool through its turn (chain with the learned turn). The policies of 2026-10-08 train on the working plant (4 N·m/rad, 0.02 s).

Seeds and evaluation

Policy quality depends on the training draw more than on the recipe. Five from-scratch draws on m05, the earlier simulation-only hand, spread their held cosine with a standard deviation of 0.38; sixteen draws there warm-started from one policy spread by 0.032; two from-scratch D6 runs of 60 M steps ended at 0.933 and 0.640 (training throughput, D6 at 60 M steps). A design comparison therefore needs several seeds or a shared warm start.

A policy is judged on jittered rollouts with a load test, under its own run’s timing. Evaluated with the residual switched on too early, the D6 policy held 12 of 64 rollouts instead of 64. Trained at one pose, D7 held 64 of 64 at that pose and 0 of 64 jittered; continuing training under jitter raised D5 from 23 to 57 and D7 from 1 to 35 of 64. Without a bound the residual commanded up to 384° past a joint’s range; clipped at ±1 rad the policies still hold 64 of 64 at the trained pose (policies across the hands, policies continued).

Fingertip contact model and transfer

Twelve D6 runs on the working plant compared four fingertip models, three seeds each, 40 M steps under a checkpoint watch that stops degenerate runs. Point-contact policies turn the tool further where they hold (cosine 0.84–0.94 against 0.61–0.79 for pads and skin) but shake it at 302 and 126 rad/s² against 18 and 19, and five of their six runs degenerated. Their finger commands keep the tool in 3 of 132 replays in other simulators; the pad and skin commands keep it in 216 of 220, and in closed loop under the other contact models the skin policies hold 99 % of rollouts and the pad policies 76 %. Every final policy grips with 40–100 N, and all but one lose the tool when the finger servo gain rises to 10 N·m/rad (contact-model policies).

Future work

  1. Replay the finger commands of the pad, skin and TPU-mesh final policies on the bench at 50 Hz from the lifted grasp, ten tracked trials each (logs/20261008-contact_model_policies/replay/). The claim fails if the TPU-mesh commands hold the tool as often as the pad or skin commands.
  2. Finetune the pad and skin policies with the grip penalty at −5 (the 2026-09-17 setting) and replay again; the replays should still hold at a grip near 10 N.
  3. Randomise the finger servo gain between 2 and 10 N·m/rad per episode in env_build.py, then evaluate at 2, 6 and 10 with rl_contact_eval.py robust. A finetuned policy that still drops the tool at 10 depends on the soft servo for its turn.
  4. Clip the residual (clip_actions 1.0) for one pad and one skin seed and compare the held cosine.
