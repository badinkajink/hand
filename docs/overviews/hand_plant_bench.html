The SR² hand, its servo model and the bench instruments

The hand has six stepper-gantry coordinates that place the finger bases and nine SCS0009 hobby servos that move the fingers. Each servo behaves as a spring, lagging its command by 0.0186° per unit of load, with a protective-torque cutoff; under a grasp the yaw joints reach 0.44–0.90 of the commanded angle. The simulated servo model in use since 6 October replays 177 tracked bench runs within 2.89° per joint but does not identify the servo gain. Two AprilTags give the tool’s pose to 0.017° and 0.030 mm.

Terms

Load
The servo’s own load reading, 0–1000 (a PWM-duty proxy for torque).
Deflection
Commanded minus achieved joint angle at a settled hold, in degrees.
Protective torque
The servo register at which it cuts its drive; the shipped value trips at a load of exactly 200.
Plant
The simulated servo model: position gain (N·m/rad), joint damping, torque limit and friction.
Readback error
Mean absolute difference between simulated and bench joint angles over the nine joints of a run, in degrees.
Time constant
Joint damping over position gain: the time a free finger takes to cover 63 % of a step.
CB1
The board that runs the servo bus and the control station; the workstation runs the camera and the tracker.

The hand

Each finger base rides on an XY gantry driven by stepper motors: the thumb’s spans 110 by 60 mm and the index and middle bases 60 by 60 mm, all at full travel since 2026-09-01. Each axis is a T5 × 1 lead screw at 3,216 steps/mm on a TMC5160 driver; a change of layout takes about 95 s, and sensorless homing (StallGuard2 against a hard stop) about 2 min. Each finger has three servo joints, a yaw at its base and two flexion joints, spanning ±85° in yaw (commands capped at ±70°), −15 to 92° at the MCP and −18 to 92° at the PIP. Seven tracked, re-staged trials of D1’s plan gave a cosine of +0.881 with a standard deviation of 0.011; the plan drifts by ±5° between sessions (program record). The links are printed; the printed TPU fingertip is a 17 by 14.8 by 22 mm block with 2.7 mm fillets. The model differs from the built hand in three measured ways: its link masses are about a quarter of the real ones, the palm plate sits 25 mm higher on the hand than it did in the model before 2026-09-16, and the yaw servos under the palm have no collision geometry (servo identification, bench partial reorientation).

Control station and the path of a plan

The CB1 board runs the control station (manta_hand.web, port 8765), the only owner of both serial links: the Manta M8P board that steps the six gantry axes over USB, and the half-duplex bus of the nine servos at 1 Mbaud. real_v1_export_plan.py writes a plan as three set-points, a 50 Hz per-joint trajectory, a build sheet and a pose sheet; a plan that passes the gates (servo range with the ±70° yaw cap, gantry travel, 5 mm of index–middle clearance along both the set-point chord and the trajectory) is copied to the CB1. During a policy stream the station writes the servos without reading them, and the tracker sends the tool pose over the network. python -m manta_hand.web --fake runs the station against simulated hardware; the operator’s guide is docs/hardware_control_station.md (program record, residual gates).

The servo: a spring and a cutoff

Over 236 logged bench runs on 11 hands, the deflection at a settled hold is 0.0186° per unit of load, one constant for every loaded joint (0.0177–0.0198, 0.94–0.99), with no stiction offset. A separate regime sits at a load of exactly 200, where the protective torque trips; it does so on 42 % of middle-yaw runs. The registers read P 15, I 0, D 15: with no integral term a standing load leaves a proportional error (servo identification).

The achieved angles therefore differ from the commanded ones. Across 20 runs the yaw joints arrived at 0.44–0.90 of their commanded travel and the distal flexion joints at 1.00 ± 0.05, for commands at 0.1 and 1.5 times the normal speed alike; the simulated rankings use commanded angles. Open-loop plans keep the tool only inside a band of residual clip per plan, and the largest clip the servos can execute is 1.345 rad for most of the family, limited by the middle finger’s distal joint (clip, cap and stall).

The simulated servo model

Three plants have been used. The shipped model (gain 30 N·m/rad) held every result before 2026-09-16. The calibrated model of 2026-09-02 (gain 0.5) came from a fit that closed the fingers on air, and its joint damping gave the fingers a 1.04 s lag. The working plant, fitted on 2026-10-06 by replaying 177 tracked bench runs, has gain 4 N·m/rad, a 0.02 s time constant, a 1 N·m torque limit and friction 1.0, and reproduces the joint readbacks within 2.89°, against 6.25° for the calibrated and 3.29° for the shipped model. The readbacks fix a time constant of 0.2 s or less and friction near 1, but not the gain: gains from 0.25 to 30 all fit within 2.53–2.75°. The same refit found that every bench scene since 2026-08-29 had started the tool 12.5 mm inside its post (servo refit).

Bench instruments

Two AprilTags, one on the palm and one on a vane on the tool, give the tool’s pose at 30 frames per second with 0.017° and 0.030 mm rms noise; the bench height equals the simulated height plus 44.0 mm (object pose from two tags). The servo bus reads the nine positions at 111 Hz once the USB adapter’s latency timer is set to 1 ms (7 Hz at its 16 ms default) and writes and reads at 75 Hz through the driver. The station reads the servos one at a time, because SYNC READ times out on every register; the servo has no current register.

On a UR5e the hand must be declared as the arm’s payload. Declared, wrist stacks of 0–150 mm complete 8 of 8 chains and the palm error at the top of the lift falls from 0.97 to 0.01 mm; undeclared, a 150 mm stack completes 0 of 8, and 23° of the turn credited to the fingers was the wrist sagging (wrist geometry).

Future work

  1. Measure the servo gain with a known load: hang 50, 100 and 200 g from a fingertip on a measured lever while the servo holds, and read the deflection; the slope is the gain in N·m/rad, which the readbacks cannot give.
  2. Measure the time constant with a 20° free-air step per joint read at 111 Hz; a value above 0.2 s contradicts the working plant.
  3. Weigh the printed links and put the real masses and centres of mass into assets/mjcf/real_v1/real_hand.xml; the model’s links are a quarter of the real mass.
  4. Look at what the tool’s handle rests on at the end of one rv05 bench run; if it is a yaw servo, add the servo bank to the model and regenerate the scenes with build_real_v1_scenes.py --keep-keyframes.
